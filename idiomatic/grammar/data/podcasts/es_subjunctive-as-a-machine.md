## SCRIPT

The Spanish subjunctive is often introduced as a list of mysterious situations: doubt, emotion, desire, uncertainty, perhaps a dozen conjunctions, and then a cloud of irregular forms. That list is accurate in pieces and useless as a system. Today we are going to replace it with a machine.

The machine has three stations. At station one, a speaker frames an idea. At station two, that framing makes a second clause dependent rather than independently asserted. At station three, Spanish marks that relationship on the dependent verb. The mark is the subjunctive.

Notice what this means. The subjunctive does not fundamentally mean that something is unreal. It means that the speaker is not presenting the dependent event as a free-standing assertion. The event may be real, likely, desired, hated, denied, merely searched for, or still pending. What unites those cases is their status inside someone else's attitude, demand, evaluation, or imagined frame.

Here is the plain assertion.

TL: Marta publica el informe hoy.

Now place the same event inside a desire.

TL: Quiero que Marta publique el informe hoy.

The publication did not become grammatically strange because we know less about it. It became subordinate to my wanting. The first verb sets the frame; que opens the chamber; publique is the machine's output.

Your Spanish record contains no captured subjunctive error. That sounds like good news, but the record also contains no visible imperfect-subjunctive attempts, while teacher-supplied triggered examples occur repeatedly. In conversational data, a construction that never appears may be mastered, or it may be avoided. So this episode does not pretend to correct an attested sentence. Its job is to make the system cheap enough to use.

Begin with the largest trigger family: influence. Wanting, requesting, requiring, recommending, permitting, preventing, and causing all treat the next action as a target, not a report.

TL: La editora quiere que cambie el título.

TL: El contrato exige que entreguemos el manuscrito en junio.

TL: No permiten que los periodistas entren.

The subjects often differ: the editor wants, I change; the contract requires, we deliver. When one person controls both verbs, Spanish usually avoids a full que-clause and uses an infinitive.

TL: Quiero cambiar el título.

TL: Quiero que cambies el título.

That is a useful diagnostic. Same actor, often infinitive. New actor, que plus a finite dependent verb, and an influence trigger normally sends that verb to the subjunctive.

Self-check one. Say, “The producer wants us to record another episode.”

[PAUSE]

TL: El productor quiere que grabemos otro episodio.

The second family is evaluation and reaction. The main clause does not report the event neutrally; it tells us how someone feels about it or judges it.

TL: Me alegra que el proyecto funcione.

TL: Es importante que los datos sean públicos.

TL: Es una lástima que no tengan más tiempo.

Again, the project may really function. The data may in fact be public. Reality is not the switch. The speaker packages the proposition as the object of joy, importance, or regret. If you remember “subjunctive equals unreal,” real reactions will always feel like exceptions. If you remember “dependent, non-asserted proposition,” they fall into place.

The third family is assertion under pressure: doubt, denial, and questions that suspend commitment.

TL: Creo que el plan funciona.

TL: No creo que el plan funcione.

The affirmative version normally asserts the plan's success and takes the indicative. The negative version withdraws that assertion and normally takes the subjunctive. But do not memorize the word creo as if it mechanically owns one mood. The speaker's stance controls the machine. A sharply incredulous question can suspend an assertion; a negative sentence can sometimes reject someone else's doubt while affirming the proposition. Mood follows the intended stance, not ink on a trigger list.

Self-check two. You reject the claim that the article is ready. Say, “I don't think the article is ready.”

[PAUSE]

TL: No creo que el artículo esté listo.

The fourth family consists of gates built into conjunctions. Some gates point toward purpose, provision, or an event still outstanding.

TL: Te mando las notas para que puedas revisarlas.

TL: Lo publicaré antes de que termine el mes.

TL: Seguiremos trabajando hasta que encontremos una solución.

Purpose is not asserted as a fact; it is the intended destination. A future event after antes de que has not yet been presented as completed. Hasta que can take either mood depending on viewpoint. If I report a completed historical sequence, I assert both events.

TL: Trabajamos hasta que encontramos una solución.

If I look ahead toward the still-unreached boundary, the dependent event remains pending.

TL: Trabajaremos hasta que encontremos una solución.

The conjunction is a signpost. Meaning still drives the car.

The fifth family concerns nouns that may not have a known, asserted referent. Compare a specific editor I know with an editor I am merely looking for.

TL: Tengo una editora que conoce ese mercado.

TL: Busco una editora que conozca ese mercado.

In the first sentence, the relative clause identifies a real member of the conversation. In the second, it defines the requirements for a possibly nonexistent person. Negation works similarly.

TL: No hay ningún programa que resuelva el problema.

The machine asks: am I identifying someone or something I assert exists, or describing the slot I hope something will fill?

Self-check three. Say, “We need a platform that protects user data,” with no particular platform identified.

[PAUSE]

TL: Necesitamos una plataforma que proteja los datos de los usuarios.

Now the forms. Present-subjunctive formation is not a second machine; it is a small output module. Take the present-tense yo form, remove its final o, and add the opposite vowel family. Verbs in ar use e endings; verbs in er and ir use a endings.

TL: publico; publique, publiques, publique, publiquemos, publiquen

TL: tengo; tenga, tengas, tenga, tengamos, tengan

TL: salgo; salga

TL: conozco; conozca

This yo-form route automatically carries many stem and spelling changes into the subjunctive. A few extremely common verbs have to be stored as landmarks.

TL: sea, esté, vaya, haya, sepa, dé

Do not chant these for five minutes. Use them inside meanings: a recommendation, a doubt, a purpose. Production becomes available by producing.

The time system is also simpler than the usual chapter layout suggests. Mood answers, “How is this proposition framed?” Tense answers, “From what time window do we view it?” A present or future frame normally selects the present subjunctive for a simultaneous or later dependent event.

TL: Espero que venga mañana.

A past or conditional frame normally shifts that dependent event into the imperfect subjunctive.

TL: Esperaba que viniera al día siguiente.

TL: Me gustaría que vinieras mañana.

The imperfect form comes from the third-person plural preterite. Remove ron and add ra endings. Tuvieron gives tuviera. Dijeron gives dijera. Fueron gives fuera. The alternative se series is also standard, but one productive series is enough to get the machine moving.

TL: tuvieron; tuviera

TL: dijeron; dijera

TL: fueron; fuera

Self-check four. Put the request in the past: “The editor asked me to shorten the chapter.”

[PAUSE]

TL: La editora me pidió que acortara el capítulo.

One more room uses the same form: contrary-to-fact conditions. The if-clause takes the imperfect subjunctive, and the result normally takes the conditional.

TL: Si tuviera más tiempo, revisaría todo el manuscrito.

This is not the ordinary open future condition. For a real possibility, Spanish uses the present indicative after si.

TL: Si tengo tiempo mañana, revisaré el manuscrito.

That contrast matters especially when Portuguese is nearby, because Portuguese productively uses a future subjunctive after future si. Spanish does not import it there. Each language sends the future condition through a different gate.

There are two bad ways to respond to this system. One is avoidance: keep every statement independent, replace wishes with quiero plus an infinitive, and never describe an unknown person. The other is trigger panic: see que and reach for the subjunctive automatically. Que is only the doorway. The first station is always meaning. Am I asserting the next proposition, or am I placing it under influence, reaction, suspended commitment, purpose, anticipation, or a hypothetical search?

Self-check five. Choose the mood by meaning. Say, “If I have time, I will call you,” as an open, realistic condition.

[PAUSE]

TL: Si tengo tiempo, te llamaré.

Here is the sixty-second recap. The subjunctive is not a fog of unreality. It is the visible output of a three-station machine. First, a main frame expresses influence, evaluation, reaction, doubt, denial, purpose, anticipation, or a nonidentified referent. Second, que or another connector makes the next proposition dependent. Third, Spanish marks that proposition with the subjunctive. Use an infinitive when the actor stays the same; use a finite clause when another subject enters. Build the present from the yo stem with opposite vowels, and build the imperfect from the preterite ellos form without ron. Keep mood and time separate. Most importantly, do not wait until the whole paradigm feels perfect. Ask the framing question and let the machine produce one meaningful sentence.
