## SCRIPT

The Spanish subjunctive has a public-relations problem. It is often introduced as a long list of emotions, doubts, wishes, conjunctions, and irregular forms. That makes it sound like a cabinet full of unrelated switches. You open the cabinet, hesitate, and choose the indicative because it feels safer.

Today, let us replace that cabinet with a machine. For the dependent clauses in this episode, use this core model: the machine marks a clause that the speaker is not presenting as a straightforward assertion. The clause may be wanted, feared, doubted, evaluated, sought, or still pending. Those meanings look different on the surface, but they all change the status of the information. The subjunctive is the machine's output signal. Spanish also uses the subjunctive in some independent wishes and commands, but those are outside today's walk.

This matters especially for you because the lesson record contains no verbatim Spanish subjunctive error to quote. That is good news, but it is not proof of spontaneous control. The record contains taught subjunctive examples and no visible imperfect-subjunctive attempts. In a corpus dominated by work and podcast narration, the easiest way never to make a subjunctive error is never to build the sentence that requires one. So this episode is about availability: making the system quick enough to use while speaking.

Begin with two boxes. Box one is the main clause. It tells us the speaker's stance. Box two is a clause introduced by the word meaning “that.” It contains the event or situation under that stance.

TL: Quiero que publiques el episodio.

Box one says, “I want.” Box two says, “you publish the episode.” The second event is not being reported as a fact. It sits inside someone's will, so the machine marks it.

Now compare a direct assertion.

TL: Sé que publicas el episodio hoy.

Here, the speaker commits to the publication as information. The indicative is the normal assertion mode. The contrast is not real versus unreal. A wish can come true, and a known fact can later prove false. The contrast is how the speaker packages the clause right now: asserted information, or information viewed through a stance.

The first input family is influence. Wanting, requesting, recommending, permitting, preventing, and insisting all put pressure on another possible action.

TL: Quiero que revises el guion.

TL: La editora recomienda que cambiemos el título.

TL: El contrato impide que vendan los derechos.

Your lesson record includes the target pattern about preventing rights from being sold.

TL: que no se vendan los derechos

Do not memorize every influence verb as an isolated magic word. Hear what the main clause does: it tries to shape the subordinate event. That event is therefore not a neutral report.

There is a useful compression rule here. If one person controls both actions, Spanish often uses an infinitive instead of opening a second clause.

TL: Quiero publicar el episodio.

If the desired actor changes, the full machine becomes visible.

TL: Quiero que publiques el episodio.

That is a tendency about clause structure, not a law that every repeated subject forbids the subjunctive. The practical question is simpler: do I have a second clause with its own subject under somebody's will?

Self-check one. You want your editor to review the manuscript. Complete the sentence.

TL: Quiero que mi editora...

[PAUSE]

TL: Quiero que mi editora revise el manuscrito.

The second input family is reaction and evaluation. Happiness, regret, fear, surprise, importance, usefulness, and judgment do not necessarily deny a fact. They put that fact under a human or impersonal evaluation.

TL: Me alegra que el episodio tenga éxito.

TL: Es importante que el audio sea claro.

TL: Temo que la audiencia pierda interés.

Notice why “subjunctive means uncertainty” is too small. In the first sentence, the episode's success may be treated as an established fact. The speaker is not uncertain; the speaker is reacting. The machine cares that the subordinate clause is framed as the object of that reaction.

The third family is doubt, denial, and withheld commitment.

TL: Creo que el plan funciona.

TL: No creo que el plan funcione.

The affirmative belief normally presents the plan's success as the speaker's view of reality. The negative belief withholds that commitment, so the machine changes output. But listen to meaning, not merely the presence of a negative word.

TL: No dudo de que el plan funciona.

This speaker says there is no doubt. The content is asserted confidently, so the indicative is natural. The machine reads the whole stance, not a nearby particle.

Your lessons have already practiced two patterns in this family: hoping that something does not disappoint us, and describing the risk that somebody may send the wrong thing. Here they are developed as complete examples.

TL: Espero que no nos desilusione.

TL: Existe el riesgo de que envíe el archivo equivocado.

Those are evidence that the pattern has been taught. Your goal now is to retrieve it before you retreat to a safer sentence.

Self-check two. You do not think the file is ready. Choose the mood and say the full sentence.

[PAUSE]

TL: No creo que el archivo esté listo.

The fourth family is purpose and pending time. A purpose is an intended outcome, not an assertion.

TL: Grabo una copia para que la editora pueda comparar las versiones.

Some conjunctions build this stance into their meaning. The phrase meaning “so that” normally opens the purpose gate, and the subjunctive follows.

Future time clauses work differently from English. When an event after “when,” “until,” or “as soon as” is still pending, Spanish normally marks that clause with the present subjunctive.

TL: Cuando termine la edición, te llamaré.

TL: No publicaré nada hasta que llegue la autorización.

TL: En cuanto tengamos los datos, prepararemos el informe.

The tense is called present, but the event can be future. Mood and clock time are separate dimensions. The present subjunctive often means simultaneous with or later than a present or future viewpoint.

Compare a habit, where the event is presented as an established pattern.

TL: Cuando termino la edición, guardo una copia.

Now compare one pending occasion.

TL: Cuando termine la edición de hoy, guardaré una copia.

Self-check three. Say, “As soon as we have the final audio, we will publish it.”

[PAUSE]

TL: En cuanto tengamos el audio final, lo publicaremos.

The fifth family is the person or thing that has not yet been identified. Compare knowing a specific editor with searching for a suitable one.

TL: Conozco a una editora que habla portugués.

TL: Busco una editora que hable portugués.

In the first sentence, the editor is a known person and her skill is asserted. In the second, the description defines the editor you hope to find. She may exist, but she has not been identified inside the conversation. The same switch appears after a negative antecedent.

TL: No hay ningún programa que resuelva este problema.

Again, “unreal” is not quite right. The program might exist somewhere. The speaker is saying that no qualifying member is available in the set under discussion.

Self-check four. You need a tool that can transcribe several languages, but you have not identified one. Say the sentence.

[PAUSE]

TL: Necesito una herramienta que pueda transcribir varios idiomas.

Some words can feed either signal because the speaker can frame the same situation in two ways. The conjunction meaning “although” is a good diagnostic. With the indicative, the speaker treats the obstacle as an established fact.

TL: Aunque el episodio es largo, mantiene el interés.

With the subjunctive, the speaker does not present the description as new, asserted information. It may be hypothetical, already known but backgrounded, or simply irrelevant to the decision.

TL: Aunque el episodio sea largo, lo publicaré completo.

The second sentence can mean “even if it is long,” but it can also concede a length both speakers already know about. This is why a trigger list eventually breaks down. A list would have to place the same conjunction in two columns. The machine asks a better question: is the description being asserted as informative, or backgrounded as hypothetical, known, or irrelevant? Mood choice can reveal that difference in stance.

Now we can reduce the machine to three questions. First: is there a subordinate clause with its own conjugated verb? Second: what stance does the main frame place over that clause? Third: am I asserting the subordinate content, or presenting it as wanted, judged, doubted, purposed, pending, or not yet identified?

The word meaning “that” does not operate the machine by itself.

TL: Sé que viene.

TL: Quiero que venga.

The verb form changes because the relationship changes.

Nor does the subjunctive belong after every word meaning “if.” In an open, realistic condition, standard Spanish uses the present indicative.

TL: Si tengo tiempo, revisaré el texto.

For a remote or counterfactual condition, Spanish uses the imperfect subjunctive.

TL: Si tuviera tiempo, revisaría el texto.

In ordinary standard Spanish, do not use a present subjunctive after conditional “if.” This is a useful firewall from Portuguese, whose future subjunctive has its own pattern after a future condition.

The imperfect subjunctive is not a second machine. It is often the same output viewed from a past, conditional, or hypothetical frame.

TL: Quiero que vengas mañana.

TL: Quería que vinieras al día siguiente.

TL: Sería útil que vinieras temprano.

The subordinate relationship remains stable while the viewpoint shifts to a past or conditional frame. For completed events, Spanish can add perfect forms.

TL: Me alegra que hayas terminado.

TL: Me alegró que hubieras terminado.

You do not need all four forms before you can choose the mood. Selection comes first. If the machine chooses subjunctive, then choose a form that fits the time relationship.

For many present-subjunctive forms, especially in the singular, a useful production path starts with the present indicative “I” form. Strip its person ending and use the subjunctive ending family. That is why many irregular first-person stems carry into the subjunctive. Stem-changing and spelling-changing verbs, especially in some plural forms, need additional adjustments, so this is a route into the paradigm, not a complete conjugation algorithm.

TL: tengo; que tenga

TL: hago; que haga

TL: conozco; que conozca

The fully irregular core is small and frequent.

TL: sea, esté, vaya, haya, sepa, dé

For the imperfect subjunctive, start from the third-person plural preterite, remove its final three letters, and add the new endings.

TL: tuvieron; tuviera

TL: hicieron; hiciera

TL: publicaron; publicara

The alternative series is also standard.

TL: tuviese; hiciese; publicase

In most ordinary contexts, the two series express the same basic mood and tense. Choose one productive series first; recognition of the other can follow.

Here is the final self-check. You are speaking hypothetically: “If we had more time, it would be useful for the editor to make another version.” Build both subjunctive clauses.

[PAUSE]

TL: Si tuviéramos más tiempo, sería útil que la editora hiciera otra versión.

If that took effort, good. The exercise forced meaning, not a naked conjugation. Repeat the sentence later at conversational speed. Production becomes available through production.

## 60-SECOND RECAP

Here is the whole walk in one minute. The indicative normally presents content as an assertion. In dependent clauses, the subjunctive marks content placed under a stance: influence, reaction, evaluation, doubt, purpose, a pending future event, or an unidentified person or thing. First find the two clauses and name their relationship. Then choose the mood; only afterward choose present, imperfect, or a perfect form. The same subject often compresses to an infinitive, while a new subject often exposes the full pattern. Remember these contrasts.

TL: Sé que viene. Quiero que venga.

TL: Cuando termino, guardo una copia. Cuando termine, te llamaré.

TL: Si tengo tiempo, lo hago. Si tuviera tiempo, lo haría.

Your record does not show repeated wrong subjunctives; it shows a system that may be easy to avoid. The goal is to let the machine run while you speak.
