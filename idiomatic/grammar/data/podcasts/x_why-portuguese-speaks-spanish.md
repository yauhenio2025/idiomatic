## SCRIPT

Imagine that Spanish and Portuguese are two colleagues who share an office, borrow each other's clothes, and answer each other's phones. Most days this is efficient. Thousands of related words and structures let you move quickly. Then Portuguese opens its mouth and Spanish answers.

This is not random confusion, and it is not evidence that you know too little. It is a predictable result of knowing several closely related languages well. When you intend a concept, your brain does not politely activate only one dictionary. Related words, sounds, endings, and constructions compete. The winner is often the form with the strongest recent history, the highest frequency, or the easiest path from meaning to speech.

Your records make that competition visible. In Portuguese, 588 of 1,098 captured error rows were classified as Romance lexical or morphological transfer. That is over half the recorded error mass. In Spanish, where the verbatim sample is much smaller, 26 of 62 pairs show cross-language interference, with roughly fourteen pointing toward Portuguese. Traffic runs both ways, but not evenly. Today we will study the mechanics so that you can catch the handoff before the wrong colleague reaches the microphone.

The first mechanism is whole-word capture. Spanish activates a familiar item, and Portuguese accepts it because it looks perfectly plausible. The following Portuguese intrusions were recorded repeatedly.

In Portuguese, the reverse pressure shows up in forms like ponemos instead of pomos, quere instead of quer, and prefirem instead of preferem. These errors reveal that you are not missing the concept of person or tense. The language label on a particular cell has become weak.

The repair is contrastive retrieval. Ask the same meaningful question twice, with a deliberate switch in language, rather than rehearsing two complete tables on different days.

Similarity is now visible, and the exact point of difference has to win attention. This feels harder than blocked practice because it is harder. That moment of discrimination is the skill you need in conversation.

The fourth mechanism is derivational overconfidence. Once you know several Romance suffixes, you can manufacture a word that looks cultivated and has never existed in the target language. Spanish examples in the record include la dictación for el dictado, la consumación for el consumo, and similaridades for las similitudes. The brain is using a productive strategy: assemble a Latinate stem and a plausible ending. Advanced speakers need that strategy. The problem is that high-frequency conventional vocabulary often resists it. For recurrent professional concepts—presentation, insertion, quality, publication—store the entire native family, not just a root.

The fifth mechanism is category capture: one language's distinction reorganizes another language's grammar. Gender is a prime example. Portuguese programa, sistema, tema, problema, and idioma are masculine, but their final a keeps inviting feminine agreement. Spanish reinforces the temptation because the cognates look almost identical—even though many of them are masculine there too. What crosses the border is less a Spanish rule than a broad visual heuristic: final a feels feminine. The Portuguese record shows the result.

TL: [pt] uma programa nova

TL: [pt] um programa novo

Another kind of category capture affects future conditions. Portuguese productively marks a pending event after quando or se with the future subjunctive.

TL: [pt] Quando eu tiver tempo, vou testar.

Spanish uses a present indicative in an open future condition after si.

TL: [es] Si tengo tiempo, lo probaré.

But Spanish often uses a present subjunctive after a future time conjunction.

TL: [es] Cuando tenga tiempo, lo probaré.

The shared meaning “future pending” does not map to one shared Romance form. That is exactly the sort of junction where one language can silently drive the other.

These are especially revealing because the wrong form often comes faster than conscious translation. That speed tells you what to monitor. A slow, unfamiliar word is already receiving attention. A high-overlap cognate does not. So use fluency itself as an alarm: when a Spanish technical word arrives with zero friction, take half a beat and ask, “Did Portuguese just answer?”

Do not perform that check on every word. Constant policing would destroy fluency. Build a watchlist from the forms that have actually crossed the border. In your case, the watchlist is small enough to name: insertar, ejemplo, largo, aislado, con nosotros, quisimos on the Spanish side; ainda, contente, tarefa, dados, apresentar, and the zero bridges after ir, tentar, conseguir, and decidir on the Portuguese side.

There is also a useful directional principle. Train the receiving language, not an abstract pair. If you repeatedly say inserir while speaking Spanish, the practice prompt should demand Spanish and present Portuguese as the distractor. If you say voy a-shaped Portuguese, the prompt should demand Portuguese and make the zero bridge the decision. Interference is asymmetric, so the treatment should be asymmetric too.

Here is the sixty-second recap. Closely related languages compete because they activate together. The intrusion can seize a whole word, a preposition bridge, a conjugated cell, a derivational pattern, or an entire grammatical category. Your Portuguese record is dominated by Spanish and Italian transfer; your Spanish record also contains a clear Portuguese stream. The answer is not to wall the languages off. Tag the handful of dangerous forms, retrieve them in meaningful contrast, and train in the direction the error actually travels. Treat suspicious ease as a brief alarm. Portuguese wants ainda, apresentar, and zero before the infinitive in vou passar and tentei instalar. Spanish wants insertar, ejemplo, largo, and quisimos. Let the languages share the office. Just make each one answer its own phone.
