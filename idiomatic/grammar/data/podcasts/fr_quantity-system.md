## SCRIPT

Welcome to episode three. Today we are going to turn a notorious French nuisance into one system. The nuisance is the cluster around de, des, du, de la, and de l apostrophe. It often gets taught as several unrelated lists: the partitive article, plural articles, contractions, quantities, and negation. That makes French feel capricious. But most choices come from two questions.

First: are you pointing to a defined thing, or merely introducing some amount of a thing? Second: has another word already measured that amount?

Those questions matter especially for you because this is the most repeated single pattern in your French record. There are one hundred and thirty-eight direct matches for the quantity error, within a broader group of one hundred and sixty-one rows. One recorded sentence was:

TL: J'apprends beaucoup des langues.

For the ordinary meaning, "I am learning many languages," the correction is:

TL: J'apprends beaucoup de langues.

That correction is not an isolated phrase to memorize. It is the visible result of the whole quantity system. Let us build that system from the ground up.

Imagine a noun arriving without an owner, without a previous mention, and without a precise number. French still wants to signal what sort of amount it is. With a singular countable object, you can introduce one item.

TL: J'ai lu un article.

With several countable objects, the ordinary indefinite plural article is des. Here des means something like "some."

TL: J'ai lu des articles.

With an uncountable substance or an amount viewed as a mass, French uses the partitive family. The form changes with the noun's gender and initial sound.

TL: Il faut du temps.

TL: Il faut de la patience.

TL: Il faut de l'argent.

These three lines all introduce an unspecified amount: some time, some patience, some money. Du is used before an ordinary masculine singular noun, de la before an ordinary feminine singular noun, and de l apostrophe before a vowel sound. At this stage, no separate word says how much.

Now add a measuring word. Beaucoup already says "a large quantity." Trop says "an excessive quantity." Assez says "a sufficient quantity." Peu, plus, moins, autant, and combien also measure. Once one of these words has done the measuring, French does not need the indefinite or partitive article to do that job again. The measuring word connects to the noun with bare de.

TL: beaucoup de temps

TL: beaucoup de patience

TL: beaucoup d'argent

TL: beaucoup d'articles

Notice what has disappeared. The masculine mass form du has become de. The feminine mass form de la has become de. The vowel form de l apostrophe has become d apostrophe. The plural indefinite des has become de. The noun can be masculine, feminine, singular, plural, countable, or uncountable. The connector does not agree. It is simply de, with elision before a vowel sound.

This is a lexical family of quantity expressions, not a rule that every number takes de. An exact cardinal number normally meets the noun directly.

TL: trois articles

A quantity noun, by contrast, can open its own de frame.

TL: une douzaine d'articles

So learn which measuring expressions select the connector. The high-value family for this fossil is beaucoup, trop, assez, peu, plus, moins, autant, and combien.

Here is your first self-check. Say, "The report contains a lot of information."

[PAUSE]

TL: Le rapport contient beaucoup d'informations.

If you produced des informations after beaucoup, hear why the extra letter is there. Des is already an indefinite plural article. Beaucoup has taken over the quantity job, so the article steps aside and bare de remains.

This also explains your second recorded fossil:

TL: Il gagne beaucoup de l'argent.

That line combines beaucoup with the ordinary partitive form de l'argent. But the intended meaning is simply that he earns a lot of money, not a large share of some already identified money. Beaucoup measures the amount, so the correction is:

TL: Il gagne beaucoup d'argent.

The same mechanism works beyond beaucoup.

TL: Nous avons trop de réunions.

TL: Le comité a assez de preuves.

TL: Cette région reçoit peu d'investissements.

TL: Le nouveau serveur consomme moins d'énergie.

TL: La rédaction demande plus de transparence.

Do not translate each expression word by word and then choose an article for the noun. Retrieve the frame as one operation: quantity word, de, noun.

Now we need to rescue des and du, because they are not mistakes. Each has more than one legitimate job. Des can be the indefinite plural article, as in des articles. It can also be the contraction of de plus les. Du can be a partitive article, as in du temps. It can also be the contraction of de plus le.

Listen to a definite example. We are not talking about arbitrary articles. We mean the particular articles you sent.

TL: J'ai lu les articles que tu m'as envoyés.

Now put beaucoup in front of that defined set. The meaning is "many of the articles you sent." The little word de means "of," and the definite article les must stay because the set is identified. De plus les contracts to des.

TL: J'ai lu beaucoup des articles que tu m'as envoyés.

This is grammatical. It does not contradict the earlier rule. The meaning has changed. Compare the pair.

TL: J'ai lu beaucoup d'articles.

TL: J'ai lu beaucoup des articles que tu m'as envoyés.

The first line introduces many indefinite articles. The second selects many members from a definite set. Bare de marks an indefinite measured quantity. De plus a definite article marks a defined subset.

The masculine singular works the same way. In the next line, du is not an unspecified partitive amount. It is the contraction of de plus le, because we are discussing the contents of one known report.

TL: J'ai vérifié la plupart du rapport.

With a feminine definite noun there is no contraction.

TL: J'ai vérifié la plupart de la traduction.

And before a vowel sound:

TL: J'ai vérifié la plupart de l'article.

So when you hear du or des, do not ask only, "What form follows this noun?" Ask what the phrase is doing. Is it introducing some amount? Or does de point into a set that is already definite?

Self-check two. You mean "many of the reports that the committee selected." Say the full phrase.

[PAUSE]

TL: beaucoup des rapports que le comité a sélectionnés

The relative clause identifies the set, so des is the contraction of de and les. If the reports were not identified, you would say beaucoup de rapports.

Negation gives us the same system from another angle. In an affirmative sentence, an indefinite or partitive object can appear with un, une, des, du, de la, or de l apostrophe.

TL: Elle a un projet.

TL: Elle a des partenaires.

TL: Elle a du temps.

But ordinary negation often turns that indefinite amount into zero. Zero is a quantity, and French normally uses de after it.

TL: Elle n'a pas de projet.

TL: Elle n'a pas de partenaires.

TL: Elle n'a pas de temps.

TL: Elle n'a pas d'argent.

Think of pas de as the zero member of the quantity family. Beaucoup de means a large amount. Peu de means a small amount. Pas de means no amount. That is much more coherent than memorizing a special article-erasing trick.

Definiteness can survive negation, however. If you deny an action involving a particular thing, its definite article remains.

TL: Je n'ai pas lu le rapport que tu m'as envoyé.

And with the verb être, French commonly retains the article because the sentence identifies or classifies what something is, rather than quantifying a direct object.

TL: Ce n'est pas du café.

The practical default is narrow and useful: when an ordinary indefinite or partitive object falls under negation, expect de or d apostrophe. Do not mechanically erase every article anywhere in a negative sentence.

Self-check three. Turn this affirmative line into "The application does not collect biometric information."

[PAUSE]

TL: L'application ne collecte pas d'informations biométriques.

There are two nearby constructions worth attaching to this map. First, autres. When you mean additional, indefinite plural things, standard French says d'autres, not des autres.

TL: Nous devons examiner d'autres options.

Here the phrase behaves like an indefinite plural under the de pattern. But des autres can be legitimate when it means "of the others" or contrasts a definite group. Again, meaning decides whether you are introducing additional things or pointing to an identified remainder.

Second, la plupart. Before a plural noun group, the ordinary frame is la plupart des. Here des reflects de plus les.

TL: La plupart des journalistes ont publié un correctif.

Before a stressed pronoun, insert entre.

TL: La plupart d'entre eux ont publié un correctif.

Your record contains the form la plupart d'eux. The repair is not merely a different article; the French chunk is d'entre eux.

There is also a formal fixed quantifier meaning "many" that retains des. Learn it as a complete expression rather than extending it to the beaucoup family.

TL: Bien des obstacles restent invisibles.

Self-check four. Say, "Most of them agree."

[PAUSE]

TL: La plupart d'entre eux sont d'accord.

One last wrinkle belongs mainly to careful written French. The indefinite plural article des is often reduced to de when an adjective comes before the noun.

TL: Le gouvernement annonce de nouvelles mesures.

TL: Le rapport avance de solides arguments.

This is useful for your formal register, but do not turn it into a universal ban on des before every adjective. Spoken and less formal usage is more variable, and fixed units can behave differently. Treat it as a careful-production pattern, separate from the extremely robust quantity rule.

Let us now turn the system into a fast decision routine. When a noun approaches, first ask whether it is definite. If it means the known report, the specified money, or the articles you sent, preserve the definite article. If de meets le or les, contract them to du or des. De la and de l apostrophe remain visibly separate.

If the noun is indefinite, ask whether a quantity word has already measured it. With no separate measure, choose the ordinary indefinite or partitive family: un or une for one countable item, des for unspecified plural items, and du, de la, or de l apostrophe for an unspecified mass. With a measure such as beaucoup, trop, assez, peu, plus, moins, or pas, use bare de or d apostrophe.

Self-check five. Choose between du and de, then say, "We have enough time to verify the figures."

[PAUSE]

TL: Nous avons assez de temps pour vérifier les chiffres.

Why de? Because assez has already measured the time. The gender of temps no longer selects the partitive du.

The goal on a walk is not to recite labels. It is to hear a sequence of jobs. Definite marker. Quantity marker. Connector. Noun. French becomes difficult when two forms look alike, especially des and du, but their jobs remain distinguishable. Des articles introduces some articles. Beaucoup des articles selects many from the articles already known. Du temps introduces some time. La plupart du temps points to most of the time under discussion. Beaucoup de temps measures an indefinite amount and uses the plain connector.

## 60-SECOND RECAP

Here is the whole system in one minute. If no separate word measures an indefinite noun, French uses its ordinary articles: un or une for one item, des for several unspecified items, and du, de la, or de l apostrophe for an unspecified mass. If a quantity word has already measured the noun, connect it with plain de: beaucoup de langues, trop de réunions, assez d'argent, peu de temps, and pas de problème. De becomes d apostrophe before a vowel sound, but it never agrees with the noun. Keep a definite article when you mean a known set: beaucoup des articles que tu m'as envoyés means many of those particular articles. In that sentence, des is de plus les; similarly, du can be de plus le. So ask two questions: is the noun definite, and has another word already expressed quantity? For your main fossil, the answer is automatic.

TL: J'apprends beaucoup de langues.
