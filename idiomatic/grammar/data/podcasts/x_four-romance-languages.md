## SCRIPT

Imagine four radio stations broadcasting from neighboring cities. They share some music, some presenters, and a great deal of vocabulary. You want Spanish, but a Portuguese word reaches the studio first. You want French, but Italian quietly edits the sentence. The result can feel like a failure to keep four languages in four sealed rooms. That diagnosis is wrong. Your brain is doing something useful too enthusiastically: it is exploiting similarity.

This walk is about how to keep the benefit and reduce the collisions. We will use your actual error record, but we will also keep the evidence honest. The Spanish profile contains sixty-two verbatim error pairs; twenty-six show cross-language or English interference. The much larger Portuguese error set classifies five hundred and eighty-eight of one thousand and ninety-eight errors as Romance interference. Those percentages are not directly comparable, because the teachers and capture systems differed. They do agree on the pattern: when a close relative offers a plausible word or structure, it often wins the retrieval race.

That race is not evidence that the languages are stored as soup. The profiles are consistent with related candidates competing during retrieval; they do not reveal how the languages are stored. A familiar-looking candidate may receive a head start because sounds, meanings, and grammatical categories overlap. That is transfer. Transfer lets you understand a new headline, guess a cognate, and recognize a subjunctive construction without beginning from zero. The very system that accelerates comprehension can also produce a form that is legal in the wrong language.

So should you separate the languages completely? Here the research gives a more interesting answer than folk advice. Pan and colleagues reported an interleaving advantage on one-week-delayed verb-conjugation tests across seven tenses in Spanish and French, including both similar and dissimilar tense pairs. In Experiment Four, mixing the two languages also improved language identification on the delayed test. Under those experimental conditions, deliberate mixing trained discrimination rather than simply making the languages melt together.

That finding has boundaries. The Experiment Four participants were English-fluent adults with no prior Romance-language experience, and the task used controlled written practice with regular verbs in only the French and Spanish present subjunctive. That population and task differ sharply from an advanced learner already managing four Romance languages. The study did not test four-language conversation, every grammar system, or an exact prescription for what to study on the same day. The research synthesis found no controlled evidence for a universal same-day schedule for cognate languages. So the honest conclusion is narrower: deliberate contrast can help you distinguish close alternatives. It does not prove that maximal chaos is optimal.

Why can interleaving work? One explanation is discriminative contrast. If you do twenty Portuguese items in a block, the language decision is made once at the beginning. Item twenty asks mainly, can you retrieve a Portuguese form? If Portuguese and Spanish alternate, every item asks two questions: which language is active, and which form belongs to it? The harder schedule practices the border itself.

Temporal spacing may also contribute to the interleaving advantage. Alternating items prevents one form from being repeated in a warm, recently activated loop. You must retrieve it after a small interruption. And the practice resembles the problem suggested by your record: several familiar alternatives are available, and sometimes the wrong one wins. That is an inference from the error pattern, not a direct test of your retrieval process. Practice that includes language selection is therefore a better match for the hypothesized bottleneck than practice that removes every competitor.

This creates a desirable difficulty. Interleaved practice can feel harder and produce more errors during training. Earlier research on Spanish preterite and imperfect found that blocking could look better during practice while interleaving did better on delayed tests. Immediate fluency can therefore be misleading. The goal is not a beautiful practice session; it is a cleaner decision tomorrow.

There is still a place for blocking. When a form is genuinely new, give it a short protected introduction. Hear it, understand it, and produce it a few times. Then bring in its competitor. For you, most of these systems are not new. The bottleneck is selecting among familiar relatives, so long protected blocks often remove the very decision you need to automate.

Grammar provides an even stronger contrast than vocabulary. Consider a future event after when. Portuguese uses the future subjunctive. Spanish normally uses the present subjunctive. Italian and French can use the future indicative. The shared meaning conceals four different instructions.

Now build a practical routine from the science and your evidence. First, name the target language before you retrieve. Not after the error: before the sentence. Second, contrast one meaning at a time. Put the Portuguese and Spanish software verbs beside each other because they compete for the same job. Third, mark the hinge in your attention: the consonant, preposition, article, or mood that identifies the language. Fourth, retrieve inside a meaningful sentence. Mechanical chanting can preserve a paradigm without training selection. Fifth, attempt the answer before hearing it. Silent retrieval is useful; the strategy evidence indicates that covert recall can support retention, which suits a walk. Sixth, make feedback specific. Say “Portuguese future subjunctive after a pending time clause,” not merely “wrong tense.”

Before each mixed prompt, give yourself a half-second routing ritual. Hear the language name, picture one diagnostic sound or construction, and only then retrieve the message. After an intrusion, do more than repeat the correction. Say the competing form once in the language where it belongs, reset the language name, and produce the target sentence twice. That sequence preserves useful knowledge while teaching it to wait for the right address.

One more self-check. In French, say, “When I return tomorrow.”

[PAUSE]

TL: [fr] Quand je rentrerai demain.

There is also an emotional adjustment here. A mixed form is not proof that studying several languages was a mistake. It shows that closely related options can compete at retrieval. The task is to add sharper routing. Treat each error as a border sign: this meaning is shared, but this particular road belongs to Portuguese; that preposition belongs to Italian; this relative pronoun tells you the sentence is French.

## 60-SECOND RECAP

Here is your sixty-second recap. Four Romance languages offer overlapping words and grammatical plans, so transfer is both your accelerator and your collision risk. Your records show the collisions clearly: a Portuguese software verb entering Spanish, a Spanish-shaped adverb entering Portuguese, pan-Romance inventions entering French, and a Spanish-looking noun or borrowed preposition entering Italian. The answer is not total separation. Research by Pan and colleagues shows that deliberate interleaving across two Romance languages can improve both conjugation learning and language identification, though it does not settle every scheduling question. Use a short block only for a truly new form, then practice contrasts. Name the language, retrieve one meaningful sentence, notice the identifying hinge, and explain the correction. If practice feels slightly slower, that may be the border becoming visible. You are teaching one multilingual mind to choose the right door.
