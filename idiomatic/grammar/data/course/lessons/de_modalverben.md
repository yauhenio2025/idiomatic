## SCRIPT

[CARD]
TITLE: Six verbs add a layer of stance
SVG: de_modalverben_c01f.svg
REF: 15, 15.1

A plain verb presents an event. A modal auxiliary places the speaker's stance in front of that event: necessity, permission, ability, preference, reported obligation, or intention. Keep the event fixed and change only that layer:
TL: Mara öffnet das Fenster.
EN: Mara opens the window.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Mara muss das Fenster öffnen.
EN: Mara has to open the window.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Mara darf das Fenster öffnen.
EN: Mara is allowed to open the window.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Mara kann das Fenster öffnen.
EN: Mara is able to open the window.
The window-opening stays the same. What changes is the force attached to it.
SHOW: event stays fixed · modal changes the speaker's stance
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: The modal is finite; the action waits
REF: 15.1, 15.1.2a, 15.1.2c

In a main clause, the modal carries person and tense in the finite-verb slot. The lexical action goes to the end as a bare infinitive, with no infinitive marker:
TL: Ich kann den Einwand nachvollziehen.
EN: I can understand the objection.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Du musst die Zahlen noch einmal prüfen.
EN: You have to check the figures again.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Wir wollen den Entwurf morgen besprechen.
EN: We intend to discuss the draft tomorrow.
The six modals share strikingly irregular singular forms, but their syntax is one system: finite modal near the front, bare infinitive at the right edge.
SHOW: finite modal in slot two · bare infinitive at the end
The next card gives that finite modal a full tense system.

[CARD]
TITLE: German modals own the tense system
SVG: de_modalverben_c02f.svg
REF: 15.1.1

English modal verbs often need paraphrases outside the present. German simply inflects the modal or builds a compound tense. That makes one contrast especially sharp: the past indicative states an actual ability; the past subjunctive makes it conditional.
TL: Gestern konnte Amir die Sitzung leiten.
EN: Yesterday Amir was able to chair the meeting.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Morgen könnte Amir die Sitzung leiten, wenn die Vorsitzende ausfiele.
EN: Tomorrow Amir could chair the meeting if the chair were unavailable.
[PAUSE:1000]
And future time uses the future auxiliary around the modal construction:
TL: Nächste Woche wird Amir die Sitzung leiten können.
EN: Next week Amir will be able to chair the meeting.
SHOW: konnte = actual past · könnte = conditional · wird … können = future
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Perfect tense: the double infinitive
SVG: de_modalverben_c02b.svg
REF: 15.1.2b, 15.1.2c

When a modal governs another infinitive, its perfect forms normally replace the past participle with a second infinitive. The closing cluster is lexical infinitive plus modal infinitive:
TL: Wir haben die Frist verlängern müssen.
EN: We had to extend the deadline.
[PAUSE:1000]
TL: Sie hätte dem Vorschlag früher widersprechen sollen.
EN: She ought to have opposed the proposal earlier.
[PAUSE:1000]
But if the modal stands alone, use its ordinary past participle:
TL: Ich habe das wirklich nicht gewollt.
EN: I really did not want that.
The presence of a dependent infinitive decides between the two shapes.
SHOW: with another infinitive: verlängern müssen · alone: gewollt
Sixteen tense and mood transformations come next.

[CARD]
TITLE: Verb clusters are built from the right
SVG: de_modalverben_c03f.svg
REF: 15.1.2c, 15.1.2e, 19.1.3

Start with the lexical action at the right. Add the modal outside it. Add a tense auxiliary outside that. In a main clause, only the outermost finite verb moves to slot two:
TL: Die Gutachterin wird den Entwurf prüfen müssen.
EN: The reviewer will have to examine the draft.
[PAUSE:1000]
In a subordinate clause with a double infinitive, the finite auxiliary does not simply go last. It stands before the two infinitives:
TL: Ich weiß, dass die Gutachterin den Entwurf wird prüfen müssen.
EN: I know that the reviewer will have to examine the draft.
SHOW: main: wird … prüfen müssen · subordinate: … wird prüfen müssen
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: The same rule survives heavier clusters
REF: 15.1.2d, 15.1.2e, 19.1.3

An infinitive clause puts the marker on the modal at the end of the cluster:
TL: Wir hoffen, die Frist verlängern zu können.
EN: We hope to be able to extend the deadline.
[PAUSE:1000]
A passive adds another infinitive without changing the architecture:
TL: Die Klausel hätte früher geändert werden müssen.
EN: The clause would have had to be changed earlier.
[PAUSE:1000]
Now place that cluster inside a subordinate clause. The finite auxiliary again steps in front of the infinitives:
TL: Es war ärgerlich, dass die Klausel früher hätte geändert werden müssen.
EN: It was annoying that the clause would have had to be changed earlier.
[PAUSE:1000]
The same placement works with a future-in-the-past:
TL: Niemand wusste, ob sie den Termin würde verschieben müssen.
EN: No one knew whether she would have to postpone the appointment.
SHOW: subordinate double infinitive → finite auxiliary before the cluster
Two densely targeted clause-building items follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: When the main verb can disappear
REF: 15.1.2f

German can omit the lexical infinitive when the missing action is recoverable. A destination can make a motion verb obvious:
TL: Ich muss morgen nach Leipzig.
EN: I have to go to Leipzig tomorrow.
[PAUSE:900]
A verb just mentioned can remain unspoken:
TL: Wir wollten mitkommen, aber wir durften nicht.
EN: We wanted to come along, but we were not allowed to.
[PAUSE:900]
And the very general action verb meaning do is often understood:
TL: Das kann ich wirklich nicht.
EN: I really cannot do that.
Omission is licensed by recoverability, not by a free choice to drop any infinitive.
SHOW: clear destination · repeated action · understood “do” → infinitive may vanish
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: German can stack two modals
REF: 15.1.2g

Standard English avoids two true modals in one clause. German does not. The inner modal belongs to the action; the outer finite modal evaluates the whole unit:
TL: Jede Redakteurin muss präzise formulieren können.
EN: Every editor has to be able to formulate things precisely.
[PAUSE:1000]
TL: Wir müssten den Seminarraum länger nutzen dürfen.
EN: We would have to be allowed to use the seminar room for longer.
[PAUSE:1000]
TL: Wie kannst du diese Entscheidung verteidigen wollen?
EN: How can you want to defend this decision?
Read from the right: formulate, be able, have to. The farther left a modal sits in the semantic stack, the wider its scope.
SHOW: action ← inner modal ← outer finite modal
Stacked modals return inside the mixed drills later in the unit.

[CARD]
TITLE: Dürfen: permission becomes prohibition
SVG: de_modalverben_c05f.svg
REF: 15.2.1a, 15.2.1b, 15.2.1d

The core of dürfen is permission. Negation removes that permission, so the result is a prohibition, not an absence of necessity:
TL: Nur autorisierte Personen dürfen den Serverraum betreten.
EN: Only authorized people are allowed to enter the server room.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Besucher dürfen den Serverraum nicht betreten.
EN: Visitors must not enter the server room.
[PAUSE:1000]
The past subjunctive softens permission into a courteous request:
TL: Dürfte ich dazu eine Rückfrage stellen?
EN: Could I ask a follow-up question about that?
SHOW: dürfen = permission · nicht dürfen = prohibition · dürfte ich = polite request
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Dürfte can estimate likelihood
REF: 15.2.1c, 15.2.2

Away from requests, dürfte often marks a well-supported expectation. It is close to probably, and it works with a simple or perfect infinitive:
TL: Die Reparatur dürfte bis Freitag abgeschlossen sein.
EN: The repair will probably be completed by Friday.
[PAUSE:1000]
TL: Der Fehler dürfte beim letzten Update entstanden sein.
EN: The error probably arose during the last update.
[PAUSE:1000]
With a past counterfactual and negation, permission returns, now as a forceful criticism:
TL: Das hätte der Ausschuss nicht beschließen dürfen.
EN: The committee ought not to have decided that.
The shared core is still what the circumstances allow.
SHOW: dürfte + infinitive = probable · hätte nicht … dürfen = should not have
Hold this contrast in mind — the mixed drills will test it later.

[CARD]
TITLE: Können: ability or possibility
REF: 15.3.1, 15.3.2

With an agent and an active infinitive, können normally means ability. Do not assume that it means perhaps just because English can uses the same word:
TL: Elena kann den Bericht ins Japanische übersetzen.
EN: Elena is able to translate the report into Japanese.
[PAUSE:1000]
Possibility becomes natural where ability makes no sense, especially with a perfect or passive infinitive:
TL: Der Bericht kann bereits veröffentlicht worden sein.
EN: The report may already have been published.
[PAUSE:1000]
The past subjunctive marks a more remote possibility:
TL: Elena könnte noch in der Bibliothek sein.
EN: Elena might still be in the library.
SHOW: agent + action → ability · perfect/passive or könnte → possibility
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Können, kennen, wissen
REF: 15.3.3, 15.3.5

As a full verb, können means mastery of something learned, especially a language, subject, tune, or practical routine. Kennen means familiarity; wissen means possession of a fact:
TL: Samir kann Arabisch und Italienisch.
EN: Samir can speak Arabic and Italian.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Samir kennt die neuen Richtlinien genau.
EN: Samir is thoroughly familiar with the new guidelines.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Samir weiß, warum die Frist verkürzt wurde.
EN: Samir knows why the deadline was shortened.
[PAUSE:1000]
And German normally states perception directly where English inserts can:
TL: Ich höre im Treppenhaus Schritte.
EN: I can hear footsteps in the stairwell.
SHOW: können = learned mastery · kennen = familiarity · wissen = fact
Ten clean choice items reinforce this three-way distinction.

[CARD]
TITLE: Mögen: liking is not wanting
REF: 15.4.1

Present-tense mögen usually means liking. Its past-subjunctive form möchte conventionally makes a polite present wish. Keep the object constant and the speech act changes:
TL: Ich mag herben Käse.
EN: I like sharp cheese.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Ich möchte den herben Käse probieren.
EN: I would like to try the sharp cheese.
[PAUSE:1000]
For an unrealized wish in the past, everyday German usually prefers gern with a past counterfactual rather than a heavy form of mögen:
TL: Ich hätte gern noch mit der Kuratorin gesprochen.
EN: I would have liked to speak with the curator as well.
SHOW: mag = likes · möchte = would like · hätte gern = would have liked
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Formal probability and concession
REF: 15.4.2, 15.4.3, 15.4.4

In formal writing, mögen can judge something reasonably possible, often while preparing a concession:
TL: Der Einwand mag berechtigt sein, aber er löst das Grundproblem nicht.
EN: The objection may be justified, but it does not solve the underlying problem.
[PAUSE:1100]
It also survives in formal concessive clauses:
TL: Wer auch immer die Daten verändert haben mag, hat Spuren hinterlassen.
EN: Whoever may have altered the data left traces behind.
[PAUSE:1000]
And its first-subjunctive form can voice a formal wish:
TL: Möge die Verhandlung zu einer fairen Lösung führen!
EN: May the negotiation lead to a fair solution!
These uses are productive, but strongly marked for register.
SHOW: mag … aber = concession · wer … mag = formal concessive · möge = formal wish
No drill set follows here — these meanings return in mixed context later.

[CARD]
TITLE: Müssen: necessity survives negation
SVG: de_modalverben_c08f.svg
REF: 15.5.1

Müssen imposes necessity. Negate the proposition, and you remove that necessity: the result is do not have to, never must not.
TL: Wir müssen einen Antrag einreichen.
EN: We have to submit an application.
[PAUSE:850]
TL: Wir müssen keinen Antrag einreichen.
EN: We do not have to submit an application.
[PAUSE:850]
TL: Wir dürfen keinen Antrag einreichen.
EN: We must not submit an application.
This polarity contrast is structural. For prohibition, German reaches for negative dürfen.
SHOW: müssen = necessary · nicht müssen = unnecessary · nicht dürfen = forbidden
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: From evidence to deduction
REF: 15.5.2, 15.5.3

Müssen can also state the conclusion forced by evidence rather than an obligation imposed on a person:
TL: Das Büro ist noch beleuchtet; Rika muss noch dort sein.
EN: The office is still lit; Rika must still be there.
[PAUSE:1000]
A negative deduction uses nicht können:
TL: Rika kann nicht zu Hause sein; ihr Fahrrad steht noch vor dem Büro.
EN: Rika cannot be at home; her bicycle is still outside the office.
[PAUSE:1000]
The past subjunctive weakens the inference into a strong expectation:
TL: Der Schlüssel müsste in der oberen Schublade liegen.
EN: The key ought to be in the top drawer.
No person is under orders here. The evidence is doing the compelling.
SHOW: muss = forced conclusion · kann nicht = impossible · müsste = expected
The mixed blocks ahead will consolidate this contrast.

[CARD]
TITLE: Wollen: intention and self-claim
REF: 15.7.1, 15.7.2

The ordinary use of wollen is active desire or intention:
TL: Wir wollen das Verfahren vereinfachen.
EN: We intend to simplify the procedure.
[PAUSE:1000]
With a perfect infinitive, the same verb can mark a claim made by the subject, often with visible speaker distance:
TL: Der Zeuge will den Fluchtwagen erkannt haben.
EN: The witness claims to have recognized the getaway car.
[PAUSE:1000]
TL: Niemand will die Warnung übersehen haben.
EN: No one admits to having overlooked the warning.
The subject owns the claim; the current speaker does not endorse it.
SHOW: will + action = intends · will + perfect infinitive = claims
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Inanimate things can resist
REF: 15.7.1b, 15.7.1c

With an inanimate subject, wollen can mean that something needs treatment or stubbornly refuses to cooperate:
TL: Der Drucker will heute nicht funktionieren.
EN: The printer refuses to work today.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Eine solche Analyse will sorgfältig vorbereitet sein.
EN: An analysis like that requires careful preparation.
[PAUSE:1000]
In a first-person plural question, wollen proposes an action the speaker favors:
TL: Wollen wir zuerst die Gegenargumente prüfen?
EN: Shall we examine the counterarguments first?
[PAUSE:800]
Compare the more open request for direction:
TL: Sollen wir zuerst die Gegenargumente prüfen?
EN: Should we examine the counterarguments first?
SHOW: inanimate will = needs/refuses · wollen wir = speaker favors the proposal
Sollen now makes that external source explicit.

[CARD]
TITLE: Sollen: obligation has a source
REF: 15.6.1, 15.6.2b

Both sollen and müssen can point toward an obligation, but sollen presents it as somebody else's instruction or expectation. Müssen presents the necessity itself:
TL: Ich soll bis Freitag eine Stellungnahme abgeben.
EN: I am supposed to submit a statement by Friday.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Ich muss bis Freitag eine Stellungnahme abgeben.
EN: I have to submit a statement by Friday.
[PAUSE:1000]
A question with sollen asks the other person to supply the decision:
TL: Soll ich das Protokoll gleich verschicken?
EN: Shall I send the minutes right away?
SHOW: sollen = sourced instruction · müssen = necessity · soll ich? = you decide
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Report, plan, destiny, condition
REF: 15.6.2, 15.6.3, 15.6.4d

Once you track the source, the wider uses of sollen line up. A report comes from unnamed others:
TL: Die Ministerin soll bereits zurückgetreten sein.
EN: The minister is said to have resigned already.
[PAUSE:900]
A planned future comes from an arrangement:
TL: Der Umbau soll im Herbst beginnen.
EN: The renovation is scheduled to begin in the autumn.
[PAUSE:900]
The past can present a later event as destiny viewed from an earlier point:
TL: Jahre später sollte er in die Küstenstadt zurückkehren.
EN: Years later he was to return to the coastal town.
[PAUSE:900]
And past-subjunctive sollen can make a remote condition:
TL: Sollten weitere Fragen auftauchen, melden Sie sich bitte.
EN: Should any further questions arise, please contact us.
SHOW: source = report · arrangement · destiny · hypothetical condition
Seven clean mixed-choice items follow now that all six modals are in play.

[CARD]
TITLE: Map the forces, not the translations
SVG: de_modalverben_c11f.svg
REF: 15.2.1, 15.5.1, 15.6.1, 15.7.1

English labels such as can, must, should, and will overlap. The reliable German system asks what relation holds between a person and an action:
TL: Du darfst die Akte lesen.
EN: You are allowed to read the file.
[PAUSE:750]
TL: Du sollst die Akte lesen.
EN: You are supposed to read the file.
[PAUSE:750]
TL: Du musst die Akte lesen.
EN: You have to read the file.
[PAUSE:750]
TL: Du willst die Akte lesen.
EN: You intend to read the file.
Permission, an instruction from elsewhere, objective necessity, personal intention: four different sources of force.
SHOW: permission · external instruction · necessity · intention
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Map the source of the statement
REF: 15.2.2, 15.3.2, 15.5.2, 15.6.3, 15.7.2

Modal auxiliaries also grade evidence or identify who is responsible for a claim:
TL: Die Ursache könnte ein defekter Sensor sein.
EN: The cause might be a faulty sensor.
[PAUSE:700]
TL: Die Ursache dürfte ein defekter Sensor sein.
EN: The cause is probably a faulty sensor.
[PAUSE:700]
TL: Die Ursache muss ein defekter Sensor sein.
EN: The cause must be a faulty sensor.
[PAUSE:900]
Those three rise from possibility to strong inference. Sollen and wollen do something different: they identify a source.
TL: Die Ursache soll ein defekter Sensor sein.
EN: The cause is said to be a faulty sensor.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Der Hersteller will den Fehler längst behoben haben.
EN: The manufacturer claims to have fixed the fault long ago.
SHOW: könnte < dürfte < muss · soll = report · will = subject's claim
Fifteen production items now test meaning rather than dictionary substitution.

[CARD]
TITLE: Scope decides what happened
SVG: de_modalverben_c12f.svg
REF: 15.3.7, 15.5.3c

English often collapses two German constructions into the same modal phrase. German keeps the scope visible. A finite modal plus a perfect infinitive judges whether a past event happened:
TL: Sie könnte den Bericht verfasst haben.
EN: She may have written the report.
[PAUSE:1000]
A past counterfactual auxiliary plus two infinitives judges an unrealized ability:
TL: Sie hätte den Bericht verfassen können.
EN: She could have written the report, but did not.
[PAUSE:1100]
The same scope contrast separates inference from missed necessity:
TL: Sie müsste den Bericht bereits verfasst haben.
EN: She ought to have written the report by now, as a reasonable inference.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Sie hätte den Bericht bereits verfassen müssen.
EN: She ought to have written the report by now, but did not.
SHOW: modal + perfect infinitive = judgment about fact · hätte + two infinitives = unrealized potential
Flip the card.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Read the cluster from the right
REF: 15.6.3, 15.6.4b, 15.7.2

The final contrast uses the same architecture. With a simple finite modal and a perfect infinitive, the speaker reports an expectation about a completed event:
TL: Er sollte die Unterlagen gestern abgeschickt haben.
EN: He was expected to have sent the documents yesterday.
[PAUSE:1000]
With a past counterfactual auxiliary and the double infinitive, the duty was not fulfilled:
TL: Er hätte die Unterlagen gestern abschicken sollen.
EN: He ought to have sent the documents yesterday, but did not.
[PAUSE:1100]
Finally, keep the source contrast intact even when both claims concern the past:
TL: Der Kurier will das Paket abgegeben haben.
EN: The courier claims to have delivered the parcel.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Der Kurier soll das Paket abgegeben haben.
EN: The courier is said to have delivered the parcel.
Read the lexical infinitive first, then the modal around it, then the finite tense or mood around the whole cluster.
SHOW: action → modal scope → tense/mood · form preserves source and evidence
