## SCRIPT

[CARD]
TITLE: A suffix can change size, attitude, or the whole word
REF: 43.1, 43.2

Spanish affective suffixes do more than make something smaller or larger. They can express warmth, contempt, irony, admiration, or intensity.
TL: Vivimos en una casita cerca del mar.
EN: We live in a little house near the sea.
[PAUSE:900]
The same diminutive can be ironic when the context contradicts literal smallness.
TL: ¡Vaya semanita!
EN: What a week!
[PAUSE:1000]
Meaning comes from the base, the suffix, the region, and the situation together.
SHOW: size · affection · understatement · irony
Flip before treating the ending as a mechanical rule.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Lexicalized forms must be learned as vocabulary
REF: 43.1

Some words that look diminutive or augmentative have acquired a stable independent meaning.
TL: ventanilla
EN: service window; vehicle window
[PAUSE:800]
TL: cajón
EN: drawer
[PAUSE:800]
TL: carrito
EN: trolley; small cart
[PAUSE:1000]
Do not infer the meaning of every suffixed form from its shape alone. If a familiar ending produces a specialized noun, store the whole word.
SHOW: transparent meaning → analyze · specialized meaning → memorize

[CARD]
TITLE: The central diminutive is -ito, with stem-sensitive variants
REF: 43.2.1

Across the Spanish-speaking world, -ito and -ita are the most widely available diminutive endings.
TL: casa → casita
EN: house → little house
[PAUSE:800]
TL: café → cafecito
EN: coffee → little coffee
[PAUSE:800]
TL: flor → florecita
EN: flower → little flower
[PAUSE:1000]
The linking material in -cito or -ecito depends on the word and variety; it is part of the learned form, not a free extra syllable.
SHOW: -ito/-ita · -cito/-cita · -ecito/-ecita
Flip for gender and regional variation.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Preserve gender, diphthongs, and the usage of a real variety
REF: 43.2.1, 43.2.6

The suffix normally marks gender, while the base keeps important internal contrasts.
TL: viejo → viejecito; vieja → viejecita
EN: old man → dear little old man; old woman → dear little old woman
[PAUSE:900]
Regional systems can prefer different formations for the same base.
TL: puerta → puertecita; puertita
EN: door → little door
[PAUSE:1000]
Recognize common variants, but produce the form established in the community you are learning from.
SHOW: gender agrees · diphthong remains · regional form is evidence

[CARD]
TITLE: Diminutives often signal stance instead of size
REF: 43.2.2

A diminutive may make a request friendlier or an amount more precise without describing a physically small object.
TL: Espere un momentito, por favor.
EN: Wait just a moment, please.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Quiero solo un poquito.
EN: I want just a little bit.
[PAUSE:900]
With relatives, names, adjectives, and adverbs, it can convey affection or intensity.
TL: La sopa está calentita.
EN: The soup is nice and hot.
[PAUSE:1000]
Read the speaker's attitude before translating the ending.
SHOW: friendly tone · affection · precision · intensity
Flip for the less neutral diminutive families.

[SIDE]
TITLE: -illo, -ín, and -uelo carry local and lexical histories
REF: 43.2.3, 43.2.4, 43.2.5

Other diminutive endings can be regional, affectionate, minimizing, humorous, or lexicalized.
TL: problemilla
EN: a minor little problem
[PAUSE:800]
TL: pequeñín
EN: little one
[PAUSE:800]
TL: callejuela
EN: narrow little street; alley
[PAUSE:1000]
Their effect is less predictable than a simple scale. Learn authentic words and phrases rather than inventing forms by analogy.
SHOW: -illo · -ín · -uelo · register and region matter

[CARD]
TITLE: Augmentatives add force, not merely dimensions
REF: 43.3

The common endings -ón, -azo, and -ote can mark large size, intensity, awkwardness, excess, or admiration.
TL: Fue un exitazo.
EN: It was a tremendous success.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Es un profesorazo.
EN: He is one heck of a teacher.
[PAUSE:900]
The derived noun may change gender.
TL: una noticia → un notición
EN: a news item → a sensational piece of news
[PAUSE:1000]
Treat the suffix as an evaluative signal whose precise force comes from context.
SHOW: size · intensity · excess · admiration
Flip for blows and negative coloring.

[SIDE]
TITLE: -azo can name an impact; pejoratives require restraint
REF: 43.3, 43.4, 43.5

With a body part or object, -azo often forms the name of a blow or sudden impact.
TL: Dio un portazo al salir.
EN: He slammed the door as he left.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Recibió un codazo sin querer.
EN: She received an accidental elbow blow.
[PAUSE:900]
Endings such as -ucho, -aco, or -ejo can add contempt or poor quality, while slang formations in -ata are unpredictable.
TL: un hotelucho
EN: a wretched little hotel
[PAUSE:1000]
Because these forms can sound comic, regional, or offensive, learn them as attested vocabulary.
SHOW: -azo impact · pejorative coloring · lexical learning

[CARD]
TITLE: Spanish spelling is systematic, but sound is not enough
REF: 44.1.2, 44.1.3

The modern alphabet has twenty-seven letters. Ch and ll represent familiar sound patterns but are not separate letters for alphabetizing.
TL: chico · llave
EN: boy · key
[PAUSE:900]
For most speakers, b and v do not contrast in pronunciation, so their spelling must be learned with the word.
TL: tubo · tuvo
EN: tube · he or she had
[PAUSE:1000]
Reliable spelling combines sound rules with lexical memory.
SHOW: 27 letters · ch/ll are sequences · b/v spelling is lexical
Flip for spelling changes that preserve a sound.

[SIDE]
TITLE: A changed letter can keep the pronunciation stable
REF: 44.1.3

Before different vowels, Spanish may change a consonant spelling to preserve the intended sound.
TL: realizar → realicé
EN: to carry out → I carried out
[PAUSE:800]
TL: buscar → busqué
EN: to look for → I looked for
[PAUSE:800]
TL: llegar → llegué
EN: to arrive → I arrived
[PAUSE:1000]
The written alternation is orthographic, not a new conjugational meaning.
SHOW: z→c before e · c→qu before e · g→gu before e

[CARD]
TITLE: First locate the default stress
REF: 44.2.1

If a word ends in a vowel, n, or s, the default stress is on the next-to-last syllable.
TL: casa · examen · hablan
EN: house · exam · they speak
[PAUSE:900]
If it ends in another consonant, the default stress is final.
TL: hablar · natural · Madrid
EN: to speak · natural · Madrid
[PAUSE:1000]
These defaults explain why most Spanish words need no written accent.
SHOW: vowel/n/s → penultimate · other consonant → final
Flip when actual stress rejects the default.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Mark exceptional stress with an acute accent
REF: 44.2.1

Write an acute accent when stress falls somewhere the ending rule would not predict.
TL: canción · lápiz · teléfono
EN: song · pencil · telephone
[PAUSE:900]
Every word stressed before the penultimate syllable therefore carries a written accent.
TL: dígamelo · pájaro
EN: tell me it · bird
[PAUSE:1000]
Monosyllables normally carry no accent unless a diacritic distinguishes another word.
SHOW: predicted stress → no mark · exceptional stress → acute accent

[CARD]
TITLE: Count adjacent vowels as one unit or as separate syllables
REF: 44.2.2, 44.2.3

The strong vowels a, e, and o normally form separate syllables beside one another. A strong vowel with unstressed i or u normally forms a diphthong.
TL: teatro · ciudad
EN: theater · city
[PAUSE:900]
An h between vowels does not itself decide the syllable boundary.
TL: prohibir · ahijado
EN: to forbid · godson
[PAUSE:1000]
Count the vowel group before applying the ordinary stress rule.
SHOW: a/e/o together → usually separate · unstressed i/u + strong vowel → usually one syllable
Flip when a weak vowel carries the stress.

[SIDE]
TITLE: A stressed i or u can break a diphthong
REF: 44.2.3

When i or u is stressed beside a strong vowel and the vowels belong to different syllables, the weak vowel receives an accent.
TL: país · día · oído
EN: country · day · heard
[PAUSE:900]
If a diphthong remains intact but the word needs an accent, the accent falls on its strong vowel.
TL: después · canción
EN: afterward · song
[PAUSE:1000]
The mark records both stress and, in the first pattern, the syllable boundary.
SHOW: stressed í/ú beside a/e/o → hiatus · intact diphthong → accent the strong vowel

[CARD]
TITLE: Question words keep their accent inside indirect questions
REF: 44.2.6

The interrogative and exclamative forms qué, quién, cuál, cómo, cuándo, cuánto, and dónde carry an accent.
TL: ¿Dónde vive Clara?
EN: Where does Clara live?
[PAUSE:900]
They remain accented when the question is embedded.
TL: No sé dónde vive Clara.
EN: I do not know where Clara lives.
[PAUSE:1000]
An unaccented relative form connects known information instead.
TL: Esta es la casa donde vive Clara.
EN: This is the house where Clara lives.
SHOW: information gap → accented · relative link → unaccented
Flip for same-sounding grammatical pairs.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Diacritical accents separate high-frequency words
REF: 44.2.7

A small set of monosyllables uses an accent to distinguish grammatical meanings.
TL: tú/tu · él/el · mí/mi · sí/si
EN: you/your · he/the · me/my · yes or self/if
[PAUSE:900]
TL: dé/de · sé/se · té/te · más/mas
EN: give/of · I know or be/se · tea/you · more/but
[PAUSE:900]
Aun means even; aún means still or yet. Solo and the demonstratives are normally written without an accent in the course standard.
SHOW: accent distinguishes grammar · learn each pair in a phrase

[CARD]
TITLE: Capitalize names, not every word associated with them
REF: 44.3.1

Use capitals at the beginning of a sentence and in proper names.
TL: Madrid · Colombia · Severo Ochoa
EN: Madrid · Colombia · Severo Ochoa
[PAUSE:900]
An adjective derived from a place or person is ordinarily lower-case.
TL: la vida madrileña · la cocina colombiana
EN: life in Madrid · Colombian cooking
[PAUSE:1000]
Official multiword names capitalize the elements that belong to the name.
TL: el Reino Unido · la Universidad de Oviedo
EN: the United Kingdom · the University of Oviedo
SHOW: proper name → capital · derived adjective → lower-case
Flip for categories that English often capitalizes.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Days, months, languages, religions, and titles are lower-case
REF: 44.3.2

Spanish normally keeps calendar words and general cultural labels lower-case.
TL: lunes, agosto, invierno, español, cristianismo
EN: Monday, August, winter, Spanish, Christianity
[PAUSE:900]
Official and personal titles are also lower-case when they accompany a name or identify an office.
TL: el presidente · la reina · el doctor García
EN: the president · the queen · Doctor García
[PAUSE:900]
In a book or film title, normally only the first word and any proper names receive capitals.
TL: Cien años de soledad
EN: One Hundred Years of Solitude
SHOW: Spanish capitals are selective · classify before capitalizing

[CARD]
TITLE: The comma marks structure, not every pause
REF: 44.4.1

In a simple list, Spanish normally omits a comma before the final y.
TL: Compramos pan, queso y fruta.
EN: We bought bread, cheese, and fruit.
[PAUSE:900]
Use a comma to detach an opening frame or an inserted comment, but not between an ordinary subject and its predicate.
TL: Después de la reunión, Ana envió el informe.
EN: After the meeting, Ana sent the report.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Mi hermano, que vive en Lima, llegará mañana.
EN: My brother, who lives in Lima, will arrive tomorrow.
SHOW: list items · opening frame · parenthetical material · never split core subject and verb
Flip for longer boundaries.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Colons announce; semicolons organize
REF: 44.4.2, 44.4.3

A colon introduces an explanation, list, quotation, or the body of a letter after its salutation.
TL: Querida Ana: Te escribo desde Sevilla.
EN: Dear Ana: I am writing to you from Seville.
[PAUSE:900]
A semicolon separates closely related clauses when a comma would be too weak, especially around longer internal lists or an adversative connector.
TL: El camino era seguro; sin embargo, decidimos esperar.
EN: The road was safe; nevertheless, we decided to wait.
[PAUSE:1000]
Choose punctuation by the relation between units, not by breath length alone.
SHOW: colon → what follows explains · semicolon → related independent units

[CARD]
TITLE: The raya frames turns and reporting clauses in dialogue
REF: 44.4.4

Spanish narrative dialogue commonly begins a speaker's turn with a long dash rather than quotation marks.
TL: —Volveré mañana.
EN: “I will return tomorrow.”
[PAUSE:900]
A reporting clause can be enclosed by dashes inside the same turn.
TL: —Volveré mañana —dijo Ana—, si no llueve.
EN: “I will return tomorrow,” Ana said, “if it does not rain.”
[PAUSE:1000]
The dash marks who is speaking and where narration interrupts the speech.
SHOW: —speech · —reporting clause— · resumed speech
Flip for quoted material inside ordinary prose.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Quotation marks belong to quotation structure
REF: 44.4.4

Publications vary between angular and curved quotation marks, and a quotation inside another quotation needs a distinct level.
TL: El informe llama al resultado «un cambio decisivo».
EN: The report calls the result “a decisive change.”
[PAUSE:900]
Single quotation marks can isolate a word being discussed.
TL: La palabra ‘esnob’ viene del inglés.
EN: The word “snob” comes from English.
[PAUSE:1000]
Follow one editorial hierarchy consistently instead of mixing shapes at random.
SHOW: outer quotation · inner quotation · word mentioned as a word

[CARD]
TITLE: Opening marks begin where the question or exclamation begins
REF: 44.4.5

Spanish writes both an opening and a closing question or exclamation mark.
TL: ¿Dónde está el informe?
EN: Where is the report?
[PAUSE:900]
If only part of the sentence is interrogative, put the opening mark at the start of that part.
TL: Marta, ¿cuándo llegarás?
EN: Marta, when will you arrive?
[PAUSE:900]
TL: El informe, ¿lo enviamos hoy o mañana?
EN: The report: shall we send it today or tomorrow?
[PAUSE:1000]
The signs map the exact span that receives interrogative intonation.
SHOW: outside context · ¿question span? · exact boundary
Flip for exclamations and forms of address.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Keep addressed names inside the marked span when they belong to it
REF: 44.4.5

A name addressed within a question or exclamation stays inside the marks with its comma.
TL: ¿Qué dices, Marco?
EN: What are you saying, Marco?
[PAUSE:900]
An exclamation can also begin after a neutral lead-in.
TL: El resultado fue, ¡qué sorpresa!, completamente distinto.
EN: The result was—what a surprise!—completely different.
[PAUSE:900]
Mixed question and exclamation force is possible, but ordinary writing usually chooses the dominant construction.
TL: ¡¿Qué has hecho?!
EN: What have you done?!
[PAUSE:1000]
Let the opening sign announce exactly where the special intonation starts.
SHOW: ¿question, name? · lead-in + ¡exclamation! · mixed force is marked
