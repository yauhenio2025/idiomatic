## SCRIPT

[CARD]
TITLE: A modal changes how an event is presented
REF: 25.1

Spanish modal verbs normally combine with an infinitive. The infinitive names the event; the finite modal adds ability, permission, obligation, probability, desire, or habit.
TL: Marta puede venir, debe llamar y suele llegar temprano.
EN: Marta can come, must call, and usually arrives early.
[PAUSE:900]
The subject belongs to the whole verb group. Only the modal is conjugated for person and tense.
SHOW: finite modal + infinitive · puede venir · debe llamar · suele llegar
Flip to identify what the modal contributes.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Start from meaning, not from one English modal
REF: 25.1, 25.7

English can, could, must, should, would, and have to do not map one-to-one onto Spanish forms. First decide whether the speaker describes a skill, an opportunity, a duty, an inference, a wish, or a repeated event.
TL: Ana sabe conducir, pero hoy no puede conducir.
EN: Ana knows how to drive, but today she cannot drive.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Debe de estar cansada, porque suele acostarse tarde.
EN: She must be tired, because she usually goes to bed late.
[PAUSE:1000]
The English surface form may be similar while the Spanish choice changes with the intended relationship.
SHOW: skill · circumstance · duty · inference · wish · habit
Flip to separate saber from poder.

[CARD]
TITLE: Saber expresses learned ability
REF: 25.2.1

Use saber plus an infinitive when someone has the knowledge or acquired skill needed to perform an action.
TL: Lucía sabe nadar y sabe preparar arroz.
EN: Lucía knows how to swim and knows how to prepare rice.
[PAUSE:900]
Saber can also take a noun or a finite clause when it means knowing a fact.
TL: Sé la respuesta, pero no sé dónde está el informe.
EN: I know the answer, but I do not know where the report is.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: saber + infinitive = know how · saber + fact = know
Flip when ability depends on the situation.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Poder expresses possibility, permission or opportunity
REF: 25.2.1

Use poder when circumstances allow or prevent the event, or when permission is at issue.
TL: Sé nadar, pero hoy no puedo entrar en la piscina.
EN: I know how to swim, but today I cannot enter the pool.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Los visitantes pueden usar esta puerta hasta las seis.
EN: Visitors may use this door until six.
[PAUSE:1000]
Ask whether the obstacle would disappear if the circumstances changed. If so, poder is usually the relevant modal.
SHOW: knowledge → saber · circumstances or permission → poder

[CARD]
TITLE: The imperfect describes an open past capacity
REF: 25.2.2

The imperfect podía describes a background ability, permission, or possibility without asserting that one particular attempt succeeded.
TL: De niño podía correr diez kilómetros sin cansarme.
EN: As a child I could run ten kilometers without getting tired.
[PAUSE:900]
The same viewpoint can explain why an event was generally possible or impossible over a period.
TL: Durante la reforma no podíamos usar la cocina.
EN: During the renovation we could not use the kitchen.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: podía = capacity or circumstances in progress · outcome left open
Flip for a bounded attempt and its result.

[SIDE]
TITLE: The preterite often reports whether the attempt succeeded
REF: 25.2.2, 25.2.3

Affirmative pudo often means managed to, while negative no pudo normally means tried or faced the opportunity but did not succeed.
TL: Al final pudo abrir la caja, pero no pudo reparar la cerradura.
EN: In the end she managed to open the box, but she could not repair the lock.
[PAUSE:1000]
With saber, the preterite commonly marks finding out or coming to know, while the imperfect describes knowledge already held.
TL: Ya sabía la verdad cuando supe quién había llamado.
EN: I already knew the truth when I found out who had called.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: podía = was able · pudo = managed · sabía = knew · supo = found out
The source exercise for this contrast is fully flagged, so no uncertain items are retained.

[CARD]
TITLE: Conditional poder makes a request less direct
REF: 25.2.3

Use podría or podrías plus an infinitive to present a request as a possibility rather than a bare command.
TL: ¿Podría repetir la última frase, por favor?
EN: Could you repeat the last sentence, please?
[PAUSE:900]
Object pronouns may precede the finite modal or attach to the infinitive.
TL: ¿Me lo podría explicar? ¿Podría explicármelo?
EN: Could you explain it to me?
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: ¿podría + infinitive? · pronouns before the modal or on the infinitive
Flip from present politeness to a past reproach.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Podía haber marks an opportunity that was missed
REF: 25.2.3

The imperfect or preterite of poder plus a perfect infinitive can reproach someone for not doing something that was possible.
TL: Podías haberme avisado antes.
EN: You could have warned me earlier.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Pudiste llamar a tu hermana cuando llegaste.
EN: You could have called your sister when you arrived.
[PAUSE:1000]
The criticism comes from context: the action was available, relevant, and left undone.
SHOW: podías haberlo hecho · pudiste hacerlo · missed opportunity
Five unflagged request and reproach prompts follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Puede que opens a subjunctive possibility
REF: 20.3.5, 25.2.3

Puede que presents a possibility without asserting it as fact, so the following finite verb is subjunctive.
TL: Puede que Elena llegue esta tarde.
EN: Elena may arrive this afternoon.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Puede que el tren esté retrasado.
EN: The train may be delayed.
[PAUSE:1000]
Do not put an infinitive after que. The subordinate verb needs its own subject and tense.
SHOW: puede que + present subjunctive
Flip when the possible event is earlier.

[SIDE]
TITLE: A completed possibility uses the perfect subjunctive
REF: 20.3.5, 25.2.4

When the uncertain event is already complete relative to now, use haya plus a participle. For an earlier past viewpoint, Spanish can use hubiera plus a participle.
TL: Puede que hayan perdido las llaves.
EN: They may have lost the keys.
[PAUSE:900]
Poder itself can also frame a speculation in a question or statement.
TL: ¿Quién habrá podido cambiar la fecha?
EN: Who could have changed the date?
[PAUSE:1000]
Keep the time relationship separate from the degree of certainty.
SHOW: current possibility → llegue · completed possibility → haya llegado
Ten clean speculation completions follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Deber presents an obligation or recommendation
REF: 25.3.1, 25.3.3

Deber plus an infinitive says that an action is required or advisable. The conditional softens the force into a recommendation.
TL: Debes entregar el formulario hoy.
EN: You must submit the form today.
[PAUSE:800]
TL: Deberías guardar una copia.
EN: You should keep a copy.
[PAUSE:1000]
In standard careful usage, obligation does not add de after deber.
SHOW: deber hacer = duty · debería hacer = advice
Flip from duty to an inference.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Deber de presents a probable explanation
REF: 25.3.2, 25.3.4

Deber de plus an infinitive marks probability or supposition. Tener que can express an even stronger inference as well as a strong external obligation.
TL: Deben de estar en casa; las luces están encendidas.
EN: They must be at home; the lights are on.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Tiene que haber una explicación más sencilla.
EN: There has to be a simpler explanation.
[PAUSE:1000]
Modern speech often drops de in inferential deber, so context remains essential; the conservative contrast keeps de for probability and omits it for duty.
SHOW: deber hacer = obligation · deber de hacer = probability · tener que = strong necessity
Six unflagged obligation and inference choices follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Tener que assigns necessity to a participant
REF: 25.3.4

Tener que agrees with the person who faces the obligation. It is common for practical, external, or unavoidable requirements.
TL: Tenemos que salir antes de las ocho.
EN: We have to leave before eight.
[PAUSE:900]
The preterite often presents the required action as actually carried out; the imperfect leaves its outcome open.
TL: Tuvieron que cancelar la reunión.
EN: They had to cancel the meeting.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: identified participant + tener que · tuvieron que often implies completion
Flip for a general rule with no named bearer.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Hay que states an impersonal necessity
REF: 25.4.2

Haber que remains third-person singular and presents a requirement generally, without assigning it to a grammatical subject.
TL: Hay que comprobar los datos antes de publicarlos.
EN: The data must be checked before publishing them.
[PAUSE:900]
Object and reflexive pronouns attach to the infinitive, not before hay.
TL: Hay que decírselo hoy y hay que prepararse bien.
EN: It must be told to them today, and one must prepare well.
[PAUSE:1000]
Keep a following reflexive pronoun in the third person: hay que levantarse, not a mixture of impersonal hay and first-person nos.
SHOW: personal → tenemos que hacerlo · general → hay que hacerlo
Five clean personal-versus-impersonal choices follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Imperfect quería leaves the outcome open
REF: 25.5.2

Quería describes an ongoing past wish or intention without telling us whether it was fulfilled.
TL: Quería hablar con la directora, pero no sé si la encontró.
EN: He wanted to speak with the director, but I do not know whether he found her.
[PAUSE:900]
When the subject changes, querer introduces que plus the subjunctive.
TL: Sus padres querían que estudiara medicina.
EN: His parents wanted him to study medicine.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: same subject → quería estudiar · new subject → quería que estudiara
Flip for the sharper meanings of the preterite.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Quise marks a bounded attempt; no quise often means refusal
REF: 25.5.2

Affirmative preterite quise commonly frames a definite attempt or decision. Context tells whether the intended action occurred.
TL: Quise llamarte, pero no encontré tu número.
EN: I tried to call you, but I could not find your number.
[PAUSE:900]
Negative preterite no quiso normally says that someone refused or deliberately did not act. Quisiera, querría, or imperfect quería can instead make a polite request.
TL: No quiso firmar. Quisiera hablar con la encargada.
EN: He refused to sign. I would like to speak with the manager.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: quería = wanted · quise = tried/decided · no quiso = refused · quisiera = polite wish
Eight unflagged querer choices follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Soler packages a recurring event
REF: 25.6

Soler plus an infinitive means usually do or be accustomed to doing. It is normally used in the present and imperfect.
TL: Suelo caminar al trabajo y solemos comer a las dos.
EN: I usually walk to work, and we usually eat at two.
[PAUSE:900]
The infinitive carries any object or reflexive pronouns just as it does after other modals.
TL: Marta suele acostarse temprano.
EN: Marta usually goes to bed early.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: present habit → suelo + infinitive
Flip for a past habit and English narrative would.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Solía and the imperfect express past habit
REF: 25.6, 25.7

Use solía plus an infinitive for a former routine. A plain imperfect often carries the same habitual viewpoint.
TL: De pequeños solíamos pasar agosto en el pueblo.
EN: As children we used to spend August in the village.
[PAUSE:900]
English narrative would can describe repeated past actions, but Spanish normally uses the imperfect rather than the conditional.
TL: Cada mañana salía a las siete y volvía al anochecer.
EN: Every morning he would leave at seven and return at dusk.
[PAUSE:1000]
Acostumbrar a can describe a person's custom, but soler is the broader neutral choice, including events such as weather.
SHOW: present routine → suele · past routine → solía or imperfect · not conditional
Seven clean habit translations follow.
