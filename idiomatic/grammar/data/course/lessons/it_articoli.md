## SCRIPT

[CARD]
TITLE: The next sound selects the definite article
REF: 4.1

First choose gender and number. For masculine nouns, use il and i before most consonants, but lo and gli before a difficult onset such as s plus consonant, z, ps, pn, gn, x, or y. Before a vowel, singular masculine uses l apostrophe and the plural uses gli.
TL: Il libro è accanto allo zaino.
EN: The book is next to the backpack.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: I libri sono accanto agli zaini.
EN: The books are next to the backpacks.
[PAUSE:1000]
Feminine singular uses la before a consonant and l apostrophe before a vowel; feminine plural always uses le.
TL: L'amica saluta le studentesse.
EN: The friend greets the students.
SHOW: il/i + ordinary consonant · lo/gli + special onset · l'/gli + masculine vowel · la or l'/le + feminine
Flip for the singular indefinite forms.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Un, uno, una, and un apostrophe follow the same sound map
REF: 4.1, 4.22

Masculine un appears before an ordinary consonant and also before a vowel. Uno appears before the same special onsets that require lo.
TL: Cerco un albergo e uno zaino leggero.
EN: I am looking for a hotel and a light backpack.
[PAUSE:900]
Feminine una appears before a consonant and elides to un apostrophe before a vowel.
TL: Ho una valigia e un'idea migliore.
EN: I have a suitcase and a better idea.
[PAUSE:1000]
The apostrophe distinguishes feminine un'idea from masculine un amico. Italian has no ordinary plural of un or una; an indefinite plural may instead use a partitive form or a bare plural.
SHOW: M ordinary or vowel -> un · M special onset -> uno · F consonant -> una · F vowel -> un'

[CARD]
TITLE: Look past meaning to the word immediately after the article
REF: 4.1

Article form depends on the opening sound of the word that comes next, even when that word is an adjective, number, or title rather than the noun.
TL: Lo studente; il bravo studente.
EN: The student; the good student.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Gli psicologi; i nuovi psicologi.
EN: The psychologists; the new psychologists.
[PAUSE:1000]
Recalculate the form whenever a modifier moves in front. Do not store lo or il as an unchangeable property of the noun.
SHOW: identify gender and number -> inspect next sound -> select article
Flip to combine a preposition with that selected form.

[SIDE]
TITLE: A, di, da, in, and su fuse with a definite article
REF: 4.2

The common prepositions a, di, da, in, and su combine with every definite-article form: al, allo, all', alla; del, dello, dell', della; dal, nel, sul, and their plural partners.
TL: Andiamo al museo e poi alla stazione.
EN: We are going to the museum and then to the station.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Il gatto dorme sul divano degli amici.
EN: The cat sleeps on the friends' sofa.
[PAUSE:1000]
Con usually stays separate in current usage, as in con il and con gli, although col remains common. Per and tra or fra normally stay separate. A title whose initial article belongs to its official name can also resist fusion.
SHOW: productive preposition + definite article -> one word · con/per/tra/fra -> usually separate

[CARD]
TITLE: Indefinite introduces; definite identifies
REF: 4.3, 4.22

Use an indefinite article to introduce one countable entity whose identity is not yet shared. Once that entity is identifiable from the conversation or situation, the definite article points back to it.
TL: Nel cortile c'è un cane. Il cane aspetta il padrone.
EN: There is a dog in the courtyard. The dog is waiting for its owner.
[PAUSE:1000]
Definiteness is about recoverable identity, not physical closeness. A unique or normally expected entity can be definite on first mention because speaker and listener can already identify it.
TL: Chiudi la porta, per favore.
EN: Close the door, please.
SHOW: new non-identified count noun -> un/uno/una · shared or recoverable identity -> definite article
Flip for whole classes and abstract ideas.

[SIDE]
TITLE: A whole class normally takes the definite article
REF: 4.4

Italian commonly uses the definite article when a noun denotes a species, substance, abstract idea, or class in general, even where English has no article.
TL: Il caffè contiene caffeina.
EN: Coffee contains caffeine.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: I bambini imparano giocando.
EN: Children learn through play.
[PAUSE:1000]
The construction with tutto also normally includes the article: tutto il giorno, tutta la settimana, tutti gli studenti, tutte le case. A bare noun can have a different, more restricted role, so do not copy the English article pattern.
SHOW: class or totality -> definite article · tutto + article + noun

[CARD]
TITLE: Body parts often use the article, not a possessive
REF: 4.5

When the possessor is clear, Italian normally treats the complete or uniquely expected body part as definite. English often expresses the same link with a possessive.
TL: Marta si lava le mani.
EN: Marta washes her hands.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Mi fa male la schiena.
EN: My back hurts.
[PAUSE:1000]
An indirect-object pronoun can identify the affected person, while the article marks the body part. Use an indefinite article when one member of a possible set matters rather than the expected whole.
TL: Il bambino ha alzato una mano.
EN: The child raised one hand.
SHOW: clear possessor + expected whole -> definite article · one non-unique member -> indefinite article
Flip for other expected possessions and conditions.

[SIDE]
TITLE: The same pattern reaches clothes, documents, and symptoms
REF: 4.5

Italian often uses a definite article with clothes, personal documents, family relations, illnesses, and other attributes that the situation makes expected.
TL: Paolo ha perso il passaporto e si è tolto la giacca.
EN: Paolo lost his passport and took off his jacket.
[PAUSE:900]
But the indefinite article changes the meaning to one item, one instance, or a newly identified member of a set.
TL: Il medico ha trovato un'infezione all'orecchio.
EN: The doctor found an infection in the ear.
[PAUSE:1000]
Ask whether the context supplies a unique expected link. Do not mechanically replace every English possessive with an article.
SHOW: contextually inherent item -> often definite · one newly singled-out item -> indefinite

[CARD]
TITLE: Countries take articles; cities usually do not
REF: 4.6, 4.7

Countries, regions, continents, and large islands generally take a definite article. With movement or location, that article is absorbed into the required preposition.
TL: Vivono in Italia, ma viaggiano spesso negli Stati Uniti.
EN: They live in Italy but often travel to the United States.
[PAUSE:900]
Cities, towns, villages, and street names normally appear without an article unless it is officially part of the name.
TL: Abito a Torino, in via Verdi.
EN: I live in Turin, on Via Verdi.
[PAUSE:1000]
After di, a singular country name can sometimes omit the article, especially when it behaves like a descriptive label.
SHOW: country/region/continent -> article · city/street -> normally zero · official name controls exceptions
Flip for rivers, lakes, and mountains.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Natural features normally keep the article
REF: 4.7

Rivers, lakes, seas, and mountains generally take the definite article. A preposition therefore combines with it when the syntax requires one.
TL: Abbiamo camminato lungo il Tevere e nuotato nel lago.
EN: We walked along the Tiber and swam in the lake.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: La neve sulle Alpi si vede da lontano.
EN: The snow on the Alps can be seen from far away.
[PAUSE:1000]
Some place names contain an article as a fixed part of the name. Learn that article with the name, because it may affect capitalization and contraction.
SHOW: river/lake/mountain -> definite article · lexicalized place name -> preserve its established form

[CARD]
TITLE: Personal names and titles follow social conventions
REF: 4.8

Ordinary given names and most masculine surnames are normally bare. An article with a surname can reflect regional usage, gendered convention, fame, historical distance, or register, so it is not a simple grammatical marker.
TL: Ho parlato con Marco e con il professor Rinaldi.
EN: I spoke with Marco and Professor Rinaldi.
[PAUSE:900]
Direct address normally omits the article before a professional title.
TL: Professore, posso farLe una domanda?
EN: Professor, may I ask you a question?
[PAUSE:1000]
When introducing yourself by surname, use the bare name. Follow current local and professional practice rather than extending an old literary pattern.
SHOW: ordinary personal name -> zero · title plus name -> usage-sensitive · direct address -> zero
Flip for names of languages.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Language names usually take an article
REF: 4.9

When a language is the topic or object in an ordinary statement, Italian commonly uses the definite article.
TL: L'italiano mi piace, ma capisco meglio lo spagnolo.
EN: I like Italian, but I understand Spanish better.
[PAUSE:900]
The article can disappear when the language is treated as a school subject, and it is normally absent after in when the phrase describes the language used.
TL: A scuola studia francese e scrive spesso in inglese.
EN: At school she studies French and often writes in English.
[PAUSE:1000]
Separate these constructions instead of treating every language name alike.
SHOW: language as topic/object -> usually definite · school subject or in + language -> often zero

[CARD]
TITLE: A bare noun can name an activity rather than an object
REF: 4.10, 4.11

Many conventional verb expressions use a bare noun because the noun contributes an activity, condition, or basic meaning rather than identifying an entity.
TL: Ho fame, ma non ho paura.
EN: I am hungry, but I am not afraid.
[PAUSE:900]
Adding an article can create an ordinary countable meaning. Fare can also combine with a definite article and a role noun to state a habitual profession or characteristic behavior.
TL: Mia sorella fa la farmacista; io faccio il cuoco.
EN: My sister is a pharmacist; I work as a cook.
[PAUSE:1000]
Learn frequent expressions as constructions, because article choice is part of their meaning.
SHOW: fixed activity or condition -> often zero · fare + definite article + role -> profession/behavior
Flip for possession and English apostrophe s.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Italian possession puts the possessed noun first
REF: 4.12, 4.13

Where English uses possessive apostrophe s, Italian normally builds article plus possessed noun plus di and the possessor.
TL: Ho trovato le chiavi del direttore.
EN: I found the director's keys.
[PAUSE:900]
The first article belongs to the possessed noun; the form after di follows the possessor. When English the points back to a previously named kind, Italian may need questo or quello instead of a bare definite article to make that anaphoric link clear.
TL: Preferisco il tè verde; quello nero è troppo forte.
EN: I prefer green tea; the black kind is too strong.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: the possessor's X -> article + X + di + possessor · contrastive the one -> questo/quello

[CARD]
TITLE: Predicative and appositive roles are often bare
REF: 4.14

A noun that classifies an already identified person by function or nature often appears without an article after essere, diventare, or similar verbs.
TL: Luca è studente e sua madre è diventata direttrice.
EN: Luca is a student and his mother became a director.
[PAUSE:900]
An appositive label behaves similarly, as do role phrases after da, come, and in qualità di.
TL: Le presento Anna Belli, responsabile del progetto.
EN: Let me introduce Anna Belli, the project manager.
[PAUSE:1000]
The indefinite article returns when the noun phrase describes one instance with additional characterization rather than merely assigning a role.
SHOW: classification or apposition -> zero · newly characterized instance -> indefinite
Flip for other environments that license zero.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Exclamations and coordinated sets can omit articles
REF: 4.15, 4.16

An adjective plus noun used as an exclamation commonly has no article. Lists can also omit repeated articles, especially when their members form a familiar pair or compact set.
TL: Povero ragazzo! Padre e figlia aspettano fuori.
EN: Poor boy! Father and daughter are waiting outside.
[PAUSE:900]
The omission presents the expressions as labels or a coordinated whole. If each noun is separately identified or contrasted, restore the appropriate article.
TL: Il padre è entrato, ma la figlia è rimasta fuori.
EN: The father went in, but the daughter stayed outside.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: exclamatory label or compact pair/list -> possible zero · separately identified members -> articles

[CARD]
TITLE: Titles and fixed prepositional phrases often use zero
REF: 4.17, 4.18, 4.19

Book, film, chapter, and section titles often appear without an article. Common phrases with a or in likewise use a bare singular noun when they name a familiar destination, location, means, or routine.
TL: Vado a scuola in autobus e torno a casa a piedi.
EN: I go to school by bus and return home on foot.
[PAUSE:900]
These are constructions, not a general rule that prepositions erase articles. A specific, modified, or contrastive place may require one.
TL: Sono entrato nella scuola nuova per parlare con la preside.
EN: I went into the new school to speak with the principal.
[PAUSE:1000]
Proverbs also preserve traditional bare-noun patterns. Learn the whole expression rather than rebuilding its articles.
SHOW: lexicalized phrase/title/proverb -> often zero · specific ordinary noun phrase -> normal article rules
Flip for unspecified quantities.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Partitive and zero present an unspecified amount differently
REF: 4.20, 4.21

Di plus a definite article forms the partitive: del pane for an unspecified amount of a mass noun, or dei libri for an unspecified plural. A bare mass noun or plural can also express an indefinite amount.
TL: Abbiamo comprato del pane e delle mele.
EN: We bought some bread and some apples.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: Vendono pane fresco e mele biologiche.
EN: They sell fresh bread and organic apples.
[PAUSE:1000]
The bare form often classifies the material or kind, while the partitive more explicitly presents a quantity. With ne and a displaced phrase, bare di can introduce the quantity domain.
TL: Di idee ne abbiamo molte.
EN: We have many ideas.
SHOW: di + article -> some quantity · zero plural/mass noun -> indefinite kind or amount · di phrase + ne -> partitive domain
