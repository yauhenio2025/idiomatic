## SCRIPT

[CARD]
TITLE: Sentence structure packages information, not just words
REF: 17.1, 17.7, 17.31

An Italian clause has grammatical relations such as subject, verb, and object, but it also has an information structure. The theme establishes what the message is about; the rheme supplies the relevant new information.
TL: Marta prepara il rapporto; il rapporto, Marta lo prepara domani.
EN: Marta prepares the report; as for the report, Marta will prepare it tomorrow.
[PAUSE:900]
The neutral order works when subject and theme coincide. A marked order, a subordinate clause, or a conditional changes how the message is framed and which relation the listener must recover.
SHOW: grammatical role + information status + clause relation

[SIDE]
TITLE: First identify the clause boundary and the controlling element
REF: 17.1, 17.8, 17.31

Ask whether you are arranging one clause, attaching a subordinate clause, or linking a condition to its result. Then identify the subject of every verb and the element that governs the dependent form.
TL: Luca promette di venire; Luca promette che Anna verrà; se Anna viene, iniziamo.
EN: Luca promises to come; Luca promises that Anna will come; if Anna comes, we begin.
[PAUSE:900]
The same English connector or infinitive can hide different Italian structures. Subject identity, factual status, and discourse focus decide among them.
SHOW: clause count -> subjects -> governing word -> information focus

[CARD]
TITLE: Neutral order places the theme before the new information
REF: 17.1

The usual declarative pattern is subject before verb and verb before its complements. Italian may instead place a new or scene-entering subject after verbs of arrival, appearance, occurrence, or sufficiency.
TL: Paolo ha aperto la porta; alla fermata è arrivato un autobus.
EN: Paolo opened the door; a bus arrived at the stop.
[PAUSE:900]
An expressed subject pronoun is normally unnecessary unless it is contrastive or otherwise informative. A postverbal noun is not automatically an object: agreement and meaning still identify its role.
SHOW: ordinary theme -> S V complements · scene + new subject -> V S

[SIDE]
TITLE: Dislocation marks a topic and normally leaves a trace
REF: 17.2, 17.4, 17.5, 17.6

Left dislocation places an object or complement first as the topic and normally resumes it with the appropriate clitic. Right dislocation states the clause first and appends an already understood element in its own intonation group.
TL: Quel messaggio, l'ho già letto; l'ho già letto, quel messaggio.
EN: That message, I have already read it; I have already read it, that message.
[PAUSE:900]
A hanging theme is looser: the following clause is complete in itself and merely comments on the announced topic. Spoken Italian can also foreground a verb as an infinitive: lavorare, lavora molto.
SHOW: left topic + clitic + clause · clause + pause + right topic · topic, complete comment

[CARD]
TITLE: Che introduces a finite subordinate clause
REF: 17.7, 17.8

Italian normally retains che where English can omit that. A finite clause is required when its expressed subject differs from the controller of the main clause.
TL: So che Giulia parte domani; voglio che voi restiate qui.
EN: I know that Giulia is leaving tomorrow; I want you to remain here.
[PAUSE:900]
When the two subjects are coreferential, Italian often prefers an infinitive. The governing verb then determines whether that infinitive is bare or introduced by a preposition.
TL: Giulia vuole partire; Giulia dice di essere pronta.
EN: Giulia wants to leave; Giulia says that she is ready.
SHOW: different subjects -> che + finite verb · same subject -> often infinitive

[SIDE]
TITLE: The governing verb selects the infinitive frame
REF: 17.9, 17.10, 17.11, 17.12, 17.13

Modals and verbs such as volere take a bare infinitive; many verbs of statement take di with a coreferential subject; smettere and finire also take di. Movement, beginning, continuation, and success verbs commonly take a.
TL: Posso entrare; ammette di aver sbagliato; ha smesso di fumare; riesce a sorridere.
EN: I can enter; she admits having made a mistake; he stopped smoking; she manages to smile.
[PAUSE:900]
With asking, ordering, allowing, or preventing, di plus infinitive can be controlled by an object of the main verb. A finite che plus subjunctive remains possible in many such frames.
TL: Gli ho chiesto di aspettare; ho chiesto che aspettasse.
EN: I asked him to wait; I asked that he wait.
SHOW: learn verb + complement pattern · do not translate the English marker mechanically

[CARD]
TITLE: A predicative adjective describes an object or result
REF: 17.19, 17.20

After verbs of believing, considering, knowing, or declaring, a noun phrase or clitic can be followed by an adjective that predicates a property of it.
TL: Ritenevano il piano impossibile; lo credevano innocente.
EN: They considered the plan impossible; they believed him innocent.
[PAUSE:900]
For an actively caused result, rendere commonly means make plus adjective. Other English resultative patterns often need a phrase that states color, completion, or the resulting event rather than a bare adjective.
TL: Hanno reso il testo più chiaro; ha dipinto la porta di rosso.
EN: They made the text clearer; she painted the door red.
SHOW: evaluation -> object + predicative adjective · caused quality -> rendere + adjective
Sixteen hygiene-gated predicative and adjective-complement items follow.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Adjectives select di, a, in, per, or da before an infinitive
REF: 17.21, 17.22, 17.23, 17.24

Feelings, emotions, certainty, and ability often take di when the adjective's subject also controls the infinitive. Aptitude, attitude, readiness, or purpose can select a, in, or per according to the adjective and meaning.
TL: Sono felice di vederti; è bravo a risolvere problemi; è pronto per partire.
EN: I am happy to see you; he is good at solving problems; he is ready to leave.
[PAUSE:900]
Da plus infinitive commonly presents the modified noun as what undergoes the infinitive's action. Do not add a passive infinitive after da.
TL: È un articolo facile da leggere; il pranzo è pronto da servire.
EN: It is an article that is easy to read; lunch is ready to serve.
SHOW: controller performs action -> adjective's lexical frame · noun undergoes action -> adjective + da + active infinitive

[CARD]
TITLE: Infinitives can encode obligation, reply, and time relation
REF: 17.25, 17.26, 17.27

Avere da plus infinitive presents something one has to do. Esserci da presents something to be done or a matter that calls for action or reflection.
TL: Ho ancora due lettere da scrivere; qui c'è da decidere subito.
EN: I still have two letters to write; here a decision must be made immediately.
[PAUSE:900]
After verbs of thought or assertion, di sì and di no answer so or not. A compound infinitive places its event before the main verb's reference point; a simple infinitive is simultaneous or later according to context.
TL: Credo di sì; penso di aver chiuso la finestra.
EN: I think so; I think I closed the window.
SHOW: task -> avere da · impersonal need -> esserci da · prior event -> auxiliary infinitive + past participle

[SIDE]
TITLE: Perception can target an event, a participant, or a fact
REF: 17.28, 17.29, 17.30

A perception verb plus noun and infinitive commonly presents the perceived event as a whole. Noun plus che plus finite verb foregrounds the participant in progress, while che before the full clause presents a perceived fact.
TL: Ho visto Paolo attraversare la piazza; ho visto Paolo che correva; ho visto che Paolo era stanco.
EN: I saw Paolo cross the square; I saw Paolo running; I saw that Paolo was tired.
[PAUSE:900]
Ecco can likewise introduce a noun plus infinitive or noun plus che plus finite verb. Choose the construction by what is directly presented, not by the English -ing form.
TL: Ecco arrivare il treno; ecco il treno che arriva.
EN: Here comes the train; here is the train arriving.
SHOW: whole perceived event -> infinitive · perceived participant in progress -> pseudo-relative · proposition -> che-clause

[CARD]
TITLE: The three central conditional patterns express different reality status
REF: 17.31, 17.32

An open condition normally uses present indicative after se and a present or future result. A present-unreal condition uses imperfect subjunctive plus present conditional.
TL: Se arriva presto, ceniamo insieme; se arrivasse presto, ceneremmo insieme.
EN: If she arrives early, we will dine together; if she arrived early, we would dine together.
[PAUSE:900]
A past-unreal condition uses pluperfect subjunctive plus past conditional. Come se takes imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive according to whether the unreal comparison concerns the present or the past.
TL: Se fosse arrivata presto, avremmo cenato insieme; parla come se sapesse tutto.
EN: If she had arrived early, we would have dined together; he speaks as if he knew everything.
Fifty-four hygiene-gated conditional items follow.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Keep protasis and result on the same time plane
REF: 17.31, 17.35

The protasis is the condition and the apodosis is its result. Determine whether the condition is open, unreal now, or unreal in the past before selecting either verb.
TL: Se hai tempo, chiamami; se avessi tempo, ti chiamerei; se avessi avuto tempo, ti avrei chiamato.
EN: If you have time, call me; if I had time, I would call you; if I had had time, I would have called you.
[PAUSE:900]
In reported past thought, the pluperfect-subjunctive and past-conditional pattern can also represent future-in-the-past rather than a rejected past possibility. Context supplies that distinction.
TL: Sapevo che, se fosse venuto, avrebbe parlato con noi.
EN: I knew that, if he came, he would speak with us.
SHOW: open -> present indicative · unreal now -> imperfect subjunctive + present conditional · unreal past -> pluperfect subjunctive + past conditional

[CARD]
TITLE: Not every se-clause is a counterfactual condition
REF: 17.33, 17.34, 17.35

In informal speech, imperfect indicative can appear in both halves of a past counterfactual, and mixed patterns occur. Recognize this register without treating it as the neutral formal template.
TL: Se lo sapevo, venivo prima.
EN: If I had known, I would have come earlier.
[PAUSE:900]
Se plus a past indicative can instead mean given that, even though, or whenever and refer to a fact. Test whether the speaker is hypothesizing or presenting an accepted circumstance.
TL: Se era stanco, non lo mostrava; se passavo di lì, salutavo sempre Anna.
EN: Even if he was tired, he did not show it; whenever I passed there, I always greeted Anna.
SHOW: hypothesis -> conditional system · accepted fact/habit -> indicative

[SIDE]
TITLE: Conditional meaning can appear without se
REF: 17.36, 17.37, 17.38

A bare imperfect or pluperfect subjunctive can introduce a counterfactual or a wish. A plus infinitive can mean if one does something in a more compressed, often evaluative frame.
TL: Avessi più tempo, viaggerei; fosse vero!; a pensarci bene, hai ragione.
EN: Had I more time, I would travel; if only it were true; if you think about it carefully, you are right.
[PAUSE:900]
An imperative followed by e or o can imply a condition and its positive or negative consequence.
TL: Premi questo tasto e il sistema riparte; sbrigati o perderai il treno.
EN: Press this key and the system restarts; hurry or you will miss the train.
SHOW: bare subjunctive · a + infinitive · imperative + e/o + consequence

[CARD]
TITLE: Choose marked order only when the discourse licenses it
REF: 17.1, 17.2, 17.3, 17.6

Start from neutral order, then ask whether a constituent is an established topic, a contrastive focus, or a recalled afterthought. Left and right dislocation normally require a matching clitic; a cleft uses essere plus the focused element and che or an infinitive.
TL: È Marta che ha chiamato; quel problema, ne parleremo domani.
EN: It was Marta who called; that problem, we will talk about it tomorrow.
[PAUSE:900]
Do not use a marked construction merely because English happens to front an element. Preserve the intended information flow and register.
SHOW: topic -> dislocation · narrow contrast -> cleft · no special focus -> neutral order

[SIDE]
TITLE: Run four checks before committing to a sentence
REF: 17.8, 17.21, 17.28, 17.31

First, identify each verb's subject. Second, identify what governs a subordinate infinitive or finite clause. Third, determine which information is given, new, or contrastive. Fourth, fix the time and reality status of every clause.
TL: Credo di capire; credo che lei capisca; quella regola, la capisco; se la capissi, la spiegherei.
EN: I think I understand; I think she understands; that rule, I understand it; if I understood it, I would explain it.
[PAUSE:900]
These checks select che or an infinitive, its preposition, neutral or marked word order, and the appropriate conditional pattern without relying on word-for-word translation.
SHOW: subjects -> governor -> information status -> time/reality -> final structure
