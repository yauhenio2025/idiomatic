## SCRIPT

[CARD]
TITLE: Personal a marks certain direct objects
REF: 26.1

Spanish normally places a before a direct object that denotes an identified person. The a does not make the object indirect; it marks how the speaker presents that direct object.
TL: Vi a la directora en la entrada.
EN: I saw the director at the entrance.
[PAUSE:900]
An ordinary non-human direct object normally appears without a.
TL: Vi el edificio desde la entrada.
EN: I saw the building from the entrance.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: identified person → a la directora · thing → el edificio
Flip to keep grammatical role separate from the marker.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Test the object before testing the a
REF: 26.1, 26.2

First locate the verb's direct object: the participant directly seen, known, invited, or affected. Then ask whether it denotes a person or familiar animal and whether that participant is identified in context.
TL: Conocemos a Marta. La conocemos desde hace años.
EN: We know Marta. We have known her for years.
[PAUSE:900]
The direct-object pronoun la confirms the role even though the full noun phrase carries a.
SHOW: conocer a Marta → la conocemos · still a direct object
Flip from the basic contrast to specificity.

[CARD]
TITLE: An identified person normally requires a
REF: 26.2

Personal names, kinship terms used for a known person, titles, possessives, and other contextually identified human objects normally take personal a.
TL: Esperamos a Elena y a su hermano.
EN: We are waiting for Elena and her brother.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: El comité entrevistó al nuevo director.
EN: The committee interviewed the new director.
[PAUSE:1000]
Each coordinated identified object keeps its own marker.
SHOW: a Elena · a su hermano · al director
Flip when the speaker is looking for a type of person, not a known individual.

[SIDE]
TITLE: An open class can appear without a
REF: 26.2

When verbs such as buscar or necesitar introduce an unspecified role or an open-ended class, a is often absent.
TL: Buscamos traductores con experiencia.
EN: We are looking for experienced translators.
[PAUSE:900]
Compare a particular person already identified by the situation.
TL: Buscamos a la traductora que llamó ayer.
EN: We are looking for the translator who called yesterday.
[PAUSE:1000]
Indefinite form alone does not settle every case; verb meaning and context can make even an indefinite human object salient.
SHOW: open requirement → buscamos traductores · identified target → buscamos a la traductora
Seven unflagged identification choices follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Names are obligatory; animals follow familiarity
REF: 26.2

A personal name used for its bearer always takes a as a direct object.
TL: Saludé a Daniel y llamé a la doctora Ruiz.
EN: I greeted Daniel and called Doctor Ruiz.
[PAUSE:900]
A pet or another familiar animal is commonly treated like an identified animate participant.
TL: El veterinario examinó a Luna esta mañana.
EN: The vet examined Luna this morning.
[PAUSE:1000]
Generic species, food, and clinical classifications are less likely to take personal a.
SHOW: person → a Daniel · familiar animal → a Luna · generic class → no automatic a
Flip for names that refer to works rather than people.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Meaning can override the shape of a name
REF: 26.2

A name can stand for an author's work, and personal a is then usual because the author remains conceptually present.
TL: Este semestre leemos a Cervantes.
EN: This semester we are reading Cervantes.
[PAUSE:900]
But a name used for a painting, product, place, or other thing follows the non-human pattern.
TL: El museo compró un Miró.
EN: The museum bought a Miró.
[PAUSE:1000]
Ask what the noun phrase denotes in this sentence, not what its spelling resembles.
SHOW: author through works → leer a Cervantes · object by creator → comprar un Miró
Flip to pronouns.

[CARD]
TITLE: Human pronouns normally carry personal a
REF: 26.4.1

Stressed and indefinite pronouns referring to people normally take a when they are direct objects: quién, alguien, nadie, cualquiera, todos, este, esa, él, ella, usted.
TL: ¿A quién invitaste? No invité a nadie.
EN: Whom did you invite? I invited nobody.
[PAUSE:900]
TL: El guardia vio a todos, pero no reconoció a ninguno.
EN: The guard saw everyone but recognized no one.
[PAUSE:1000]
This pattern includes pronouns such as alguien and nadie even though they do not identify a named individual.
SHOW: a quién · a alguien · a nadie · a todos
Flip for the short object pronouns.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Unstressed object pronouns do not add a
REF: 26.4.1

The short object forms me, te, lo, la, nos, os, los, and las stand directly before the verb or attach where the verb form permits. Do not put personal a before them.
TL: A Clara la vi ayer, pero hoy no la he llamado.
EN: Clara I saw yesterday, but today I have not called her.
[PAUSE:1000]
In the left-dislocated phrase a Clara, the full noun carries the marker. The resumptive pronoun la keeps its ordinary clitic position.
SHOW: vi a Clara · la vi · a Clara la vi
Flip for relative clauses, where ambiguity matters.

[CARD]
TITLE: Human relative objects can show a explicitly
REF: 26.4.2

When a relative pronoun is the direct object and refers to a person, a quien, al que, or a la que can display both the object relationship and personal a.
TL: La médica a quien consultamos trabaja aquí.
EN: The doctor whom we consulted works here.
[PAUSE:900]
These forms are especially useful when bare que could be mistaken for the subject of the relative clause.
TL: Esos son los autores a los que siempre critican.
EN: Those are the authors whom they always criticize.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: person as object → a quien / al que / a la que
Flip for restrictive clauses with bare que.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Bare que is possible when the structure is clear
REF: 26.4.2

In a clearly restrictive clause, bare que can refer to a human direct object without carrying a itself.
TL: Vi a varios alumnos que no conocía.
EN: I saw several students whom I did not know.
[PAUSE:900]
The antecedent already has a before varios alumnos; the relative que is separate. Choose an expanded form when the clause would otherwise be unclear or when the non-restrictive structure calls for it.
SHOW: antecedent → a varios alumnos · restrictive relative → que no conocía
The workbook's entire relative-pronoun set is judgment-flagged, so it is not retained.

[CARD]
TITLE: Tener changes pattern with possession and state
REF: 26.6

Possessive tener normally introduces what someone has without personal a, even when the noun denotes a person.
TL: Julia tiene dos hijos y un perro.
EN: Julia has two children and a dog.
[PAUSE:900]
When tener presents an identified participant in a temporary state or activity rather than simple possession, a is usual.
TL: Julia tiene a los niños esperando en el coche.
EN: Julia has the children waiting in the car.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: possession → tiene dos hijos · participant in a state → tiene a los niños esperando
Flip for querer.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Querer separates wanting a thing from loving a person
REF: 26.6

Querer plus a thing or event means want and takes no personal a.
TL: Quiero café y quiero descansar.
EN: I want coffee and I want to rest.
[PAUSE:900]
Querer plus an identified person commonly means love or feel affection for and takes a.
TL: Quiero mucho a mi abuela.
EN: I love my grandmother very much.
[PAUSE:1000]
Meaning and animacy work together; do not translate the English verb in isolation.
SHOW: want → quiero café · love → quiero a mi abuela
Flip for parallel comparisons.

[CARD]
TITLE: Parallel human objects repeat the marker
REF: 26.3, 26.8

When two human direct objects are compared or coordinated, repeat a so that both relationships remain visible.
TL: Admiro a Inés más que a su jefe.
EN: I admire Inés more than her boss.
[PAUSE:900]
A noun linked by como to an identified human object also normally carries a when it describes how that person is treated or regarded.
TL: Trataron a Pablo como a un invitado de honor.
EN: They treated Pablo as a guest of honor.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: a Inés más que a su jefe · a Pablo como a un invitado
Flip when two different a phrases occur together.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Two a phrases can mark two different roles
REF: 26.8

With verbs such as presentar, both the human direct object and an indirect object can begin with a. Clitic doubling or word order may reveal which participant has which role.
TL: Le presenté a Lucía al director.
EN: I introduced Lucía to the director.
[PAUSE:900]
The clitic le points to the recipient, while a Lucía is the person introduced. When context does not make the roles clear, rewrite the sentence rather than relying on a alone.
SHOW: le = recipient · a Lucía = direct object
Flip for places and ordinary things.

[CARD]
TITLE: Ordinary inanimate objects reject personal a
REF: 26.9, 26.10

A straightforward non-living direct object appears without personal a.
TL: Visitamos el museo y fotografiamos la fachada.
EN: We visited the museum and photographed the façade.
[PAUSE:900]
Place names also normally appear without a after verbs such as visitar, ver, abandonar, pintar, or gobernar when the place itself is meant.
TL: Visitamos Lima y después abandonamos la ciudad.
EN: We visited Lima and then left the city.
[PAUSE:1000]
SHOW: visitar el museo · visitar Lima · no personal a
Flip for personification and structural ambiguity.

[SIDE]
TITLE: Context can animate or disambiguate an object
REF: 26.5, 26.9, 26.10

A collective noun may take a when it stands for people, and a personified object may behave like an animate participant.
TL: El entrenador elogió al equipo después del partido.
EN: The coach praised the team after the match.
[PAUSE:900]
Spanish can also use a with a non-living object to prevent confusion between subject and object, especially in marked structures. These are extensions of the core pattern, not permission to add a to every definite thing.
SHOW: people behind a collective → a may appear · ordinary thing → no a
Eight clean general corrections follow.

[CARD]
TITLE: Use a decision sequence, not one keyword
REF: 26.1, 26.2, 26.4.1

First identify the direct object. Next decide what it denotes in context. Then ask whether a human or familiar animal is identified, particularized, or represented by a human pronoun.
TL: Necesitamos técnicos. Necesitamos a los técnicos del turno de noche.
EN: We need technicians. We need the technicians from the night shift.
[PAUSE:1000]
Finally check whether the construction has its own contrast, as with tener, querer, a comparison, a relative clause, or an ambiguity.
SHOW: role → meaning → animacy → identification → construction
Flip for the limits of the rule.

[SIDE]
TITLE: The core is stable even when usage varies
REF: 26.2, 26.5, 26.10

Speakers vary at the edges: indefinite people, animals, collectives, personification, and ambiguity can shift with viewpoint and register. The safest productive core is firm.
TL: Conozco a Ana. Busco a mi médico. Veo el coche.
EN: I know Ana. I am looking for my doctor. I see the car.
[PAUSE:900]
Do not force a binary shortcut onto a context-sensitive marker. Master the obligatory center first, then interpret variation by what the speaker treats as individuated and animate.
SHOW: obligatory center first · contextual edges second
