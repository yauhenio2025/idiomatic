# Genus bleibt, Kasus bewegt sich

The article in *der Plan, die Agentur, das Programm* is not disposable packaging. It is part of the lexical entry. **Gender belongs to the noun; case belongs to the noun phrase’s current job.** A feminine noun does not become masculine when its article changes from *die* to *der*. It remains feminine in *mit der Agentur*.

That distinction matters because German noun phrases are assembled in two stages. First retrieve the noun with its gender and plural: *das Ziel, die Ziele*. Then ask what role this particular phrase plays. Case changes the article and agreement around the noun; it does not rewrite the noun’s gender.

## Store nouns in usable form

Learn *Ziel* as **das Ziel, die Ziele**, not as an isolated sound. For professional vocabulary, keep a small “danger shelf” whose German gender conflicts with a strong association from another language:

> **das** Ziel · **das** Programm · **die** Agentur · **der** Verlag · **das** Publikum · **die** Regierung

Some endings are genuinely useful. Nouns in *-ung, -heit, -keit, -schaft, -ion, -tät, -ik* and *-ur* are normally feminine: *die Regierung, die Öffentlichkeit, die Möglichkeit, die Gesellschaft, die Redaktion, die Universität, die Politik, die Agentur*. Diminutives in *-chen* and *-lein* are neuter; nouns in *-ment* and many in *-um* are neuter: *das Mädchen, das Büchlein, das Parlament, das Publikum*.

These are prediction tools, not permission to guess forever. Exceptions and borrowed words still need lexical storage. The compound’s final element normally controls gender: *das Weltbild* because *das Bild*; *die Literaturagentur* because *die Agentur*; *der Buchmarkt* because *der Markt*.

## The case map

The definite article gives a compact orientation table:

| case | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nominative | der | die | das | die |
| accusative | den | die | das | die |
| dative | dem | der | dem | den (+ noun *-n* where possible) |
| genitive | des (+ noun *-(e)s*) | der | des (+ noun *-(e)s*) | der |

Use syntax and government, not English word order alone.

- **Nominative** marks the subject and a predicate noun after *sein, werden* or *bleiben*: *Diese Agentur ist ein seriöser Verlagspartner.*
- **Accusative** marks the object of many transitive verbs: *Die Redaktion prüft das Programm.*
- **Dative** marks certain recipients and is selected by certain verbs and prepositions: *Die Redaktion sagt dem Programm, welche Passage es hervorheben soll.*
- **Genitive** builds relationships especially common in written prose: *die Reichweite des Programms*.

> **You specifically do this — Du machst genau das:** The corpus preserves *Meine ultimative Ziel*, *den Programm* and *Dieser Modelagentur ist nicht seriös*. Restore the lexical genders before choosing case: **mein ultimatives Ziel** (*das Ziel*, nominative), **dem Programm** (*das Programm*, dative after *sagen*), **diese Modelagentur** (*die Agentur*, nominative subject). The surface forms differ because the jobs differ, not because the nouns changed gender.

## Role before position

German can front an object without turning it into a subject:

> **Diesen neuen Entwurf** kritisiert die Presse.  
> **Diesem neuen Programm** fehlen klare Quellenangaben.

In the first sentence, *die Presse* performs *kritisieren* and *diesen neuen Entwurf* receives the action: the overt masculine accusative ending removes the ambiguity that a neuter form would leave. In the second, *fehlen* gives the thing lacked as nominative (*klare Quellenangaben*) and the affected entity as dative (*diesem neuen Programm*). Articles and endings preserve the roles when word order moves.

Pronouns and agreement help confirm the analysis: *Das Programm ist teuer. **Es** wird trotzdem gekauft.* Neuter *es* points back to *das Programm*. For a female professional, referential gender and grammatical word formation align naturally in *die Presseagentin; sie*. Say *Sie war deine Presseagentin*, not *deine Presseagent*, when the referent is a woman and that distinction is relevant.

## Countries expose both systems

Most country names appear without an article: *nach Italien fliegen; in Italien arbeiten*. Country names that conventionally take an article keep it throughout the paradigm. *Die Türkei* therefore gives direction with accusative and location with dative:

> Die Journalistin fliegt **in die Türkei**.  
> Die Journalistin recherchiert **in der Türkei**.

> **You specifically do this — Du machst genau das:** *nach Türkei* was corrected repeatedly. The problem is not simply the preposition. Retrieve the country as **die Türkei**, then let the directional *in* phrase select accusative: **in die Türkei**.

The direction/location contrast belongs to spatial uses of two-way prepositions. It does not override a verb’s fixed preposition and case; chapter 07 treats that separately.

## A noun-phrase checksum

Before accepting a phrase, read it outward from the noun:

1. What is the stored gender and plural?
2. What syntactic role or governing word selects the case?
3. Does the determiner display that gender-case combination?
4. Do any adjectives agree with that same package?

For *mit einem neuen Programm*, the stored noun is neuter, *mit* requires dative, *einem* marks dative neuter, and the adjective therefore takes *-en*. This checksum turns four apparent choices into one coherent structure.
